Assessment 2

XR Experience Analysis & Interaction Prototype

Author

Mr. John Jennings

Overview

In this assessment, you will critically analyse an existing VR or AR application that you have used yourself, then rebuild one key interaction from it as a small working prototype in Unity.

This task combines analysis, design, and implementation, allowing you to demonstrate your understanding of XR systems and interaction design.

Item Detail
Weighting 30%
Type Case study and prototype
Submission Individual
Due Week 8 (reading week)

Learning Outcomes

  • LO2: Critique and deconstruct XR experiences
  • LO3: Demonstrate practical competence with XR systems
  • LO4: Apply HCI principles in immersive environments

The Two Rules

Important1. Analyse anything you can use hands-on

You may analyse any type of VR or AR application (a game, a training simulation, a creative tool, a fitness app, a phone AR app, a system menu, and so on), as long as it runs on equipment available from TUS or on your own equipment, and you have used it yourself. An analysis based only on videos or reviews is not accepted.

2. Build the prototype in Unity

Your prototype must be a Unity VR or AR application running on one of the supported devices below. Other tools (Unreal, Godot, WebXR, Lens Studio, Reality Composer, and so on) are not accepted for the prototype, even where the original application was built with them.

The application you analyse and the device you build for do not have to be the same. For example, you can analyse a HoloLens 2 demo and rebuild its hand-ray interaction on a Quest 3, or analyse an iPhone AR app and rebuild its placement interaction on an Android phone. Explain the change of platform in your evaluation.

Choose an application whose interactions you can describe clearly and that has at least one interaction small enough to rebuild. The table below shows the range of application types you can choose from. The titles are examples only; any application that meets Rule 1 is acceptable.

Tip

Some applications are excellent to analyse but too large to rebuild, for example location-based AR such as Google Maps Live View, or multiplayer social VR. That is fine: analyse the whole application, and rebuild one small interaction from it.

Your choice of application must be agreed with me at the Week 6 practical (the Assessment 2 clinic). Bring the name of the application, the device you used it on, and the interaction you plan to rebuild.

Task

Part 1 — Experience Analysis

Analyse the application you chose, using the frameworks from Weeks 3–6:

  • Interaction design: Norman’s Action Cycle and the Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation
  • Presence and immersion: Slater’s Place Illusion and Plausibility Illusion
  • Spatial design: position on Milgram’s continuum, placement of UI and content
  • Comfort and usability: locomotion, reach, fatigue, simulator sickness, accessibility
  • Strengths and weaknesses, judged against heuristics with severity ratings

Focus on two or three key interactions rather than the whole application, and say which one you will rebuild and why.

Support your analysis with:

  • Screenshots, photos or short recordings that you captured yourself
  • Diagrams (for example, an action-cycle table for each key interaction)
  • References to lecture concepts and readings

Part 2 — Interaction Prototype

Rebuild one key interaction from the application as a Unity VR or AR application.

Your prototype must:

  • Be built in Unity, using the XR Interaction Toolkit and/or AR Foundation (or the Meta XR SDK or Cardboard XR Plugin where appropriate)
  • Run on a real device from the equipment table
  • Be interactive and functional: the user’s input causes a clear system response
  • Give feedback to the user: visual, plus audio and/or haptic where the device supports it
  • Demonstrate an understanding of XR input (controllers, hands, gaze, touch) and output (display, audio, haptics)

A focused prototype is expected. You are not expected to recreate the original’s art, levels, menus, scoring, music or story. Primitive shapes (cubes, spheres, capsules) and free assets are fine; credit any assets you did not make.

Part 3 — Evaluation

Evaluate your prototype:

  • What works well?
  • What doesn’t?
  • How does it compare to the original? Where it differs, is that a limitation of your build, of the platform, or a deliberate design change?
  • What would you improve, and why?

Base your evaluation on evidence. As covered in the Week 6 lecture, combine an inspection method (a heuristic evaluation) with a small user test: SUS is the recommended minimum, and add the SSQ (before and after) if your prototype involves locomotion. Use the consent form and SSQ from Week 5. Three to five participants is enough; be honest about what a small sample can and cannot show.

Scoping Your Prototype

The most common way to lose marks in this assessment is to choose an interaction that is too big and submit something that does not work. A small interaction that works well will score higher than an ambitious one that is broken.

NoteRule of thumb

If you cannot describe the interaction in one sentence, and build a first working version in two lab sessions, cut it down.

Suggested interactions by level

All of the interactions below can be built with the standard XR Interaction Toolkit or AR Foundation components and the skills from the Weeks 2–5 labs. Start at the Core level and add to it only once it works on the device.

Stretch: only if your Core version is already working on the device

  • Comparing two versions of the interaction (for example ray versus direct grab) in your user test
  • Placing content on the Quest 3 scene model (walls, tables) in passthrough
  • A custom hand gesture beyond the toolkit’s built-in pinch and grab
  • Directional slicing (the hit only counts if the controller moves in the indicated direction)

Avoid

These are out of scope for a small project and are a common cause of unfinished submissions:

  • Multiplayer or networking
  • Persistent, shared or cloud anchors, and location-based (GPS) AR
  • Full-body avatars or inverse kinematics
  • Recreating a whole game loop: levels, scoring, menus, save systems
  • AI-driven characters or procedural content
  • Custom shaders or advanced visual effects

Two worked examples

  • VR: Beat Saber
  • AR: Furniture placement

Interaction chosen: hitting an incoming block with a saber.

Prototype: a cube spawns every two seconds and moves towards the player. The right controller holds a thin stretched capsule as the saber. When the saber collides with a cube, the cube is destroyed, a hit sound plays and the controller gives a short haptic pulse. A world-space text counter shows the number of hits.

Left out on purpose: music, beat-synced spawning, slicing direction, menus and scoring.

Evaluation angle: how much does haptic and audio feedback contribute to the feeling of a “hit”? Test it with feedback on and off.

Interaction chosen: placing a piece of furniture on the floor and adjusting it.

Prototype: AR Foundation detects horizontal planes. A placement indicator follows the centre of the screen across the detected floor. Tapping places a simple chair model, built from primitives, at the indicator. Dragging moves it and a two-finger twist rotates it.

Left out on purpose: a product catalogue, real-world scale checking and saving or sharing.

Evaluation angle: do users understand when the floor has been detected, and where the object will appear? Compare your placement indicator with the original’s.

Submission

Submit:

  • Report (1500–2000 words) covering Parts 1 and 3, with a short description of how you built Part 2
  • Unity project, zipped, excluding the Library, Temp, Logs and Build folders
  • Prototype build for your target device (an .apk for Quest, Pico or Android; a Windows build for PC VR; for iOS, the video is accepted in place of a build)
  • Video demo (2–3 minutes) showing the prototype running on a real device: a headset capture or phone screen recording, ideally alongside footage of you using it

Marking Criteria

Component Weight What is assessed
Analysis 30% Depth and critical quality of the analysis of the original application; correct use of the Weeks 3–6 frameworks; evidence you gathered yourself
Prototype 30% The interaction works reliably on a real device; it clearly recreates the chosen interaction; appropriate use of XR input, output and feedback. Scope and visual polish are not marked: a well-built Core interaction can score full marks
HCI Application 20% Design decisions in the prototype are justified with HCI principles (feedback, affordances, comfort, reach, accessibility)
Evaluation 10% Evidence-based evaluation: heuristic review and user testing, a fair comparison with the original, and realistic improvements
Presentation 10% Clear structure and writing, labelled figures, correct referencing, and a clear video

Tips

  • Be critical, not descriptive: “the menu is on the wrist” is description; why it works or fails is analysis
  • Focus on interaction design
  • Link your work to module concepts
  • Get the interaction working on the device early, then improve it
  • Keep your prototype simple but functional
