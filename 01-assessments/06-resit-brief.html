Resit Brief

Combined Assessment 2 and 3: New XR Analysis, Interaction Prototype and Mini Game

Author

Mr. John Jennings

Overview

This brief is for students who need to resit Assessment 2 and Assessment 3 together. It replaces both with one combined submission.

You will analyse an existing VR or AR application, build one interaction from it, extend that interaction into a small, complete mini game (a vertical slice), and evaluate the result with real users.

ImportantThe concept must be new

Your resit must be built on a new concept and a new core interaction. It must not be based on anything you submitted for Assessment 2 or Assessment 3, or on any earlier attempt at either. See What “new” means below.

Item Detail
Replaces Assessment 2 (30%) and Assessment 3 (40%)
Weighting 70% of the module (30% + 40%)
Type Case study, prototype and individual project
Submission Individual
Due See Moodle Assignment for the resit due date and time

If you also need to resit Assessment 1 (continuous lab work), you will be given a separate brief for it.

Learning Outcomes

  • LO1: Critical understanding of 3D systems and AR/VR principles in a spatial computing context
  • LO2: Explore, critique and deconstruct modern VR, AR and spatial computing experiences
  • LO3: Theoretical understanding of, and practical competence with, advanced input/output devices and sensors
  • LO4: Apply HCI principles to the construction and evaluation of immersive systems

What “new” means

To count as new, your resit must meet all of these conditions:

  1. A different application. You analyse an application that is not the one you analysed in Assessment 2.
  2. A different core interaction. Your core mechanic must be in a different interaction category from your Assessment 2 and Assessment 3 work. For example, if you previously built a throw or grab interaction, a different throwing game is not new. Choose something such as a socket, a measure tool, a wrist menu or a draw tool instead. The categories are listed in the table in Choosing a new concept.
  3. A different game. Your mini game is not a variation, reskin or extension of a game you built before.
  4. Fresh work. You start a new Unity project. Do not reuse code, scenes, prefabs, scripts, artwork, audio or written text from your earlier submissions.

You may reuse:

  • skills and techniques you learned in the labs
  • the Unity packages and sample assets the module provides (for example XR Interaction Toolkit samples), provided you credit them
  • lessons learned from your earlier feedback. Say so in your reflection.
WarningEvidence of originality

Your work will be checked against your earlier submissions. Reusing earlier work is treated as an academic integrity issue. Your repository history, development log and concept approval (see Submission) are part of the evidence that this is new work.

The Rules

The same practical rules as Assessment 2 apply. Read them in the Assessment 2 brief.

Important1. Analyse anything you can use hands-on

You may analyse any VR or AR application that runs on equipment available from TUS or on your own equipment, as long as you have used it yourself. An analysis based only on videos or reviews is not accepted.

2. Build the project in Unity

Your project must be a Unity VR or AR application running on a real device from the Assessment 2 equipment table. Other engines are not accepted. You can develop and debug in the Meta XR Simulator or the XR Interaction Simulator, but your video must show the project running on a real device.

Choosing a New Concept

Choose an application with at least one interaction that is small enough to rebuild. Then plan a mini game around that interaction.

The table shows the interaction categories and some mini game ideas. The ideas are examples only. Your concept must be in a category you did not use in Assessment 2 or 3.

Interaction category Core interaction (the prototype) Example mini game (the vertical slice)
Grab, move, throw Pick up an object and release or throw it Bowling alley, target-toss or a recycling-sorting game
Ray select Point a ray at a world-space panel and press a button A pattern-matching or memory panel game
Socket / snap-in Place an object in a slot to trigger a change A circuit-building or potion-mixing puzzle
Hit a moving target Strike or shoot incoming targets with feedback A whack-a-mole or reflex-training game
Draw in 3D Leave a trail while a button is held A trace-the-shape challenge with a score
Wrist or palm menu A menu appears when the palm turns up A cooking or tool-belt game where items are chosen from the menu
Two-handed rotate and scale Grab a model with both hands to rotate and resize it A 3D jigsaw or part-assembly challenge
Measure (AR) Tap two points on real surfaces and show the distance An AR estimation game: guess a length, then measure it
Tap-to-place (AR) Place an object on a detected plane A timed AR layout or decorating challenge
Image-triggered content (AR) A model appears on a printed image An AR treasure-hunt or poster-based quiz
Gaze-and-dwell A reticle fills while you look at a target A 360° observation or hidden-object game (Cardboard)
Teleport / locomotion Move between points with an arc and marker A short navigation or obstacle course

Your concept must be agreed with me in writing before you start building (see Concept approval).

Tip

Some applications are excellent to analyse but too large to rebuild, such as location-based AR or multiplayer social VR. That is fine: analyse the whole application and rebuild one small interaction from it.

Task

The task has three linked parts. They are all about the same new concept.

Part 1: Experience Analysis

Analyse the application you chose, using the frameworks from Weeks 3–6:

  • Interaction design: Norman’s Action Cycle and the Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation
  • Presence and immersion: Slater’s Place Illusion and Plausibility Illusion
  • Spatial design: position on Milgram’s continuum, placement of UI and content
  • Comfort and usability: locomotion, reach, fatigue, simulator sickness, accessibility

Focus on one or two key interactions and say which one you will rebuild and why. Support your analysis with screenshots, photos or recordings you captured yourself, and with at least one diagram (for example an action-cycle table).

Part 2: Interaction Prototype and Mini Game

Build the project in two stages.

Stage A: Core interaction. Rebuild one key interaction from the application. It must be reliable on a real device and give clear feedback to the user.

Stage B: Vertical slice. Extend the interaction into a short, complete and playable mini game of about 2–3 minutes. Work through these layers in order, and stop when you run out of time:

  1. Make the core reliable.
  2. Add a goal and a loop: a clear start, a challenge, and an end or result.
  3. Add multi-modal feedback: visual effects, spatial audio and haptics (where the device supports them) working together.
  4. Add essential UI: instructions, score or progress, and a results state, placed with comfort in mind.
  5. Polish: lighting, animation and performance on device.

Primitive shapes and free assets are fine. Credit any assets you did not make.

WarningKeep the scope small

A small mini game that works beats an ambitious one that is broken. The “Avoid” list from Assessment 2 still applies: no multiplayer or networking, no persistent, shared or cloud anchors, no full-body avatars, no AI-driven characters, no large content pipelines.

If you cannot describe your mini game in two sentences, cut it down.

Part 3: Evaluation

Because there is no earlier prototype to compare against, you show that evaluation changed your work by testing twice:

  • Round 1: test the core interaction (end of Stage A, or the first playable version) with at least three participants.
  • Improve the build using what you found.
  • Round 2: test the improved mini game with at least three participants. They may include people from Round 1.

In each round, every participant completes:

  • the SSQ before and after, so you can report the change
  • the SUS after

Use the consent form, the SSQ and the SUS. Report SUS scores and SSQ change for each round, with your observations. Show what you changed between the rounds, and why. Be honest about what a small sample can and cannot show.

Concept Approval

Before you start building, submit a short concept proposal through the route given on Moodle. It must include:

  • the application you will analyse and the device you used it on
  • the core interaction you will rebuild and its interaction category
  • a two-sentence description of your mini game
  • the target device for your build
  • the interaction category, application and game you used in Assessment 2 and Assessment 3, so I can confirm that the new concept is different

Do not begin building until you have my written approval. Work started without approval may be rejected as not meeting the brief.

Milestones

Exact dates are on Moodle. These are checkpoints, not separate submissions.

Start of resit period Concept proposal sent and approved

Deadline Final submission

Submission

Submit:

  1. Report (3000–3500 words) covering the analysis, design decisions, a short technical description, both evaluation rounds, and a critical reflection
  2. Unity project, zipped, excluding the Library, Temp, Logs and Build folders
  3. Build for your target device (an .apk for Quest, Pico or Android; a Windows build for PC VR; for iOS the video is accepted in place of a build)
  4. Video demo (3–4 minutes) showing the mini game running on a real device, ideally alongside footage of you using it
  5. Evaluation evidence: consent forms (with names removed), completed SSQ and SUS forms or summaries, and your observation notes
  6. Repository link and development log: a link to your Git repository with commit history from the resit period, and a short log of what you built and when
  7. Originality declaration: a short statement confirming that the concept, code and written work are new, and listing any third-party assets

Report structure

  1. Application analysis (Part 1)
  2. Concept and design decisions, with reference to module concepts, including how feedback is combined across visual, audio and haptic channels
  3. Technical implementation
  4. Evaluation: method, Round 1 findings, changes made, Round 2 findings
  5. Critical reflection and further work, including what you learned from your earlier attempt
NoteWhy one report?

The report is shorter than the two original reports added together. The analysis is focused on one or two interactions, and the testing replaces the comparison with an earlier prototype.

Marking Criteria

Component Weight What is assessed
Experience analysis 15% Depth and critical quality of the analysis of the original application. Correct use of the Weeks 3–6 frameworks. Evidence you gathered yourself
Technical implementation 25% The interaction and mini game run reliably on a real device. Competent use of the toolchain. A complete, playable loop with no blocking bugs. Appropriate scope
Interaction and experience design 25% Deliberate design choices justified with HCI principles (feedback, affordances, comfort, reach, accessibility). Coherent multi-modal feedback. Clear UI and a clear start and end
Evaluation 20% A real method, honestly applied, with SUS and SSQ in two rounds. Findings that visibly changed the work
Critical reflection 5% Engages with module theory. Honest about limitations. Shows what was learned since the earlier attempt
Presentation and documentation 10% Clear structure and writing, labelled figures, correct referencing, a clear video, and complete supporting documents

A submission that is not based on a new concept, as defined above, cannot pass the Technical implementation and Interaction design components.

Marking Bands

Band Descriptor
70–100 Controlled and reliable. Analysis is critical and uses the module frameworks. Design choices are explicitly grounded in module theory. Both evaluation rounds are genuine and demonstrably changed the work. Reflection is self-aware.
60–69 Solid, working mini game with clear design reasoning. Evaluation carried out competently in both rounds. Reflection shows good understanding.
50–59 Application runs with limitations. Design reasoning present but thin. Evaluation minimal or only one round. Reflection descriptive.
Below 50 Incomplete or non-functional. Little design rationale, no meaningful evaluation, or the concept is not new.

Tips

  • Get approval first. A concept that is too similar to your earlier work will be sent back.
  • Start fresh and start early. Get a simple version running on the device in the first week.
  • Keep the interaction small. Build the loop before the effects.
  • Test early. Round 1 is meant to find problems while you can still fix them.
  • Test with someone who has not seen it. You cannot evaluate your own interface.
  • Write the report as you go. Design decisions are impossible to reconstruct honestly at the end.
  • Report what did not work. An honest evaluation of a flawed build scores better than a claim that everything worked.
