Audio is routinely the last thing added to an XR project and one of the cheapest ways to raise presence. This session treats sound and touch as primary design materials. The lab wires spatial audio and haptics to the Assessment 3 core interaction, which is demonstrated on device this week.

Why Audio Matters

  • A headset shows about 100° of the world; hearing covers all 360°
  • Sound turns heads: the most reliable way to direct attention to something behind the user (Week 9)
  • It carries through walls and works in the dark
  • It confirms contact, weight and material that the visuals only suggest
  • It is cheap: a well-placed spatialised sound costs far less than the visual detail it can replace

Yet audio is usually the last thing added.

How We Locate Sounds

The brain locates a sound from three cues:

  • ITD (interaural time difference): the sound reaches the nearer ear a fraction of a millisecond earlier
  • ILD (interaural level difference): the head shadows the far ear, especially at high frequencies
  • Pinna cues: the folds of the outer ear filter sound differently from above, below, in front and behind

An HRTF (head-related transfer function) recreates all three, so headphone audio can place a sound behind or above the listener: binaural rendering.

Spatialisers in Unity

Every frame, the spatialiser takes each source’s position relative to the user’s head and renders it binaurally.

Audio Source › Spatialize On

Use mono clips for spatialised sources: stereo clips cannot be placed at a point.

Attenuation

The attenuation curve sets how volume falls with distance, and so how far away a sound feels.

  • Logarithmic rolloff is realistic but falls off fast: sounds vanish sooner than expected
  • Linear rolloff is predictable and suits gameplay cues
  • Custom curves keep important sounds audible where the user will actually be
  • Min distance: full volume inside it. Max distance: where attenuation stops.

Tune curves by ear, in the headset, not by numbers in the Inspector.

Reverb and Acoustic Space

  • Reverb tells the ear how big a space is and what it is made of
  • Small hard rooms: short, bright reflections. Large halls: long, rich decay. Outdoors: almost none.
  • Match reverb to what the user can see: a mismatch breaks Plausibility Illusion (Week 6)
  • In Unity: Audio Reverb Zone presets, or room acoustics in the Meta XR Audio SDK
  • In passthrough MR, the real room’s acoustics already apply to real sounds: match virtual sounds to them

Diegetic and Non-Diegetic Sound

The same distinction as diegetic UI in Week 5:

Diegetic Non-diegetic
Source In the world Not in the world
Examples Radio, footsteps, a door Music score, narrator, UI beeps
Rendering Spatialised Head-locked stereo
Effect Builds presence Can pull the user out

A UI sound can become diegetic: play the click from the button, not from inside the head.

A Vocabulary for Haptics

Controller haptics are a small vocabulary, used like sound design:

  • Amplitude — how strong: a light touch vs a heavy impact
  • Frequency — how fast it vibrates: a low rumble vs a sharp buzz (support varies by device and API)
  • Duration — how long: a tick vs a sustained vibration

Design from the physical cause: what would this contact feel like if it were real? A single “buzz” for every event teaches the user nothing.

Event and Continuous Haptics

State Haptic Type
Hover Tiny tick Event
Select / grab Firm click Event
Hold and move Low vibration that follows speed or contact Continuous
Release Soft pulse Event

In Unity (XR Interaction Toolkit 2.x): set Haptic Events on the interactor in the Inspector, or call SendHapticImpulse(amplitude, duration) from interactable events.

Hand tracking has no haptics: lean harder on audio and visuals.

Synchronised Feedback

  • The brain fuses sight, sound and touch into one event when they arrive within a few tens of milliseconds
  • Out of sync, the same three cues feel like three separate events, and the contact feels fake
  • Trigger visual, audio and haptic feedback from the same interaction event, in the same frame
  • Avoid audio clips with silence at the start: trim them so the sound starts on the first sample
  • Check on device: latency differs between the editor and the headset

Cross-Modal Illusions

Pseudo-haptics use vision and sound to imply forces the controller cannot apply (Lécuyer, 2009):

  • Weight: a heavy object lags slightly behind the hand (a control/display ratio below 1). Users read the lag as mass.
  • Texture: small vibrations and sounds that follow movement across a surface
  • Impact: a sharp sound and a strong, short pulse at the moment of contact

Combine cues: each one makes the others more convincing.

Where the Illusion Breaks

Problem Instead
Hand passes through a wall Stop the virtual hand at the surface, with a buzz on contact
Sound doesn’t match the material Match the sound to what the object looks like
Haptic with no visible cause Always show what caused it
The same buzz for everything Vary by event and weight
Late audio or haptic One event, same frame
Constant strong rumble Save strength for moments that matter

Key Takeaways

  • Audio is the highest-yield presence technique on this hardware: design it, don’t add it last
  • Spatialisers recreate ITD, ILD and pinna cues; use mono sources and match reverb to the room
  • Decide for every sound whether it belongs in the world
  • Haptics are a vocabulary of amplitude, frequency and duration; map each pattern to a cause
  • A convincing interaction is a visual event, a sound and a haptic arriving together

Discussion

  • If audio gives so much presence for so little cost, why is it so often added last? What would change if it were designed first?
  • When is non-diegetic music the right choice in XR, despite its presence cost?
  • Hand tracking has no haptics. Should hand-tracked experiences avoid interactions that depend on touch, or compensate with audio and visuals?
  • Pseudo-haptic weight works by making the virtual hand disagree with the real one. Where is the line between a useful illusion and a frustrating loss of control?
  • How would you design audio and haptic feedback for a user who is deaf or hard of hearing?
