Weeks 1–6 treated the headset mostly as a display and an input device. This week is about how it models the room it is in, and how content gets a durable place inside that model. The lab puts this straight into practice with AR Foundation tap-to-place.

What Makes It Spatial?

Spatial computing adds two things to VR and simple AR:

  • Awareness — the device keeps a live model of the room: floor, walls, furniture
  • Persistence — content keeps its place in that room: across head movement, restarts and days

The mechanisms:

  • Tracking tells the device where it is
  • Scene understanding tells it what the room is like
  • Anchors tell content where it belongs

Inside-Out 6DoF Tracking

  • 6DoF = 3 axes of position + 3 of rotation (yaw, pitch, roll)
  • Inside-out: the sensors are on the headset, not mounted in the room
  • Cameras find visual features and estimate position
  • IMU (accelerometer + gyroscope) measures fast motion between camera frames
  • Sensor fusion combines the two: the IMU is fast but drifts; the cameras correct it

Quest 2/3, HoloLens 2 and phone AR all use this approach. The Vive Pro uses external base stations (outside-in).

SLAM in Outline

Simultaneous Localisation And Mapping: work out where you are while building the map you need to do it.

  1. Detect features — distinctive points: corners, edges, texture
  2. Estimate pose — how the device has moved since the last frame
  3. Update the map — add new features as more of the room is seen
  4. Loop closure — recognise a place seen before and correct accumulated drift
  5. Relocalise — after tracking is lost, match the view to the saved map

When Tracking Breaks

Condition Why What the user sees
Low light Too few visible features Drift, then “tracking lost”
Blank walls, glossy floors No texture to track Content slides
Repeating patterns Features look alike Pose jumps
Covered cameras, fast motion Frames unusable Freeze, then snap back
Moved furniture Map no longer matches Relocalisation fails

Design for it: show tracking state, guide the user to look around, and never leave them in a frozen view.

Scene Understanding

The device’s model of the room comes in layers:

  • Planes — flat surfaces: floor, walls, table tops
  • Mesh — a triangle surface over everything, including irregular objects
  • Semantic labels — what a surface is: floor, wall, ceiling, table, couch

Measured vs declared geometry

  • Measured: detected live by the cameras and depth sensor
  • Declared: captured once in a guided room setup (Quest 3 Space Setup) and stored

Plane Detection & Raycasting

The core loop of this week’s lab, in AR Foundation:

  1. ARPlaneManager detects planes and adds them to the scene
  2. ARRaycastManager casts a ray from the tap (or controller) against those planes
  3. Place a prefab at the hit pose: position and rotation, so it sits flat on the surface

The rig is an XR Origin with an AR Session (older tutorials call it AR Session Origin).

Spatial Anchors

An anchor is a pose the device commits to tracking against the real world.

Kind Survives Typical use
Local This session only Placed objects during one visit
Persistent App restart, another day A virtual screen that stays on your wall
Shared Another user’s device Co-located multi-user experiences

In AR Foundation: ARAnchorManager. Persistence and sharing depend on the platform: Meta, ARCore and ARKit each do it differently.

Anchor Drift & Its Limits

  • An anchor is the device’s best estimate, refined as it sees more of the room
  • A small error in an anchor’s rotation becomes a large position error far away from it
  • Keep content close to its anchor; give separate objects their own anchors
  • Large spaces: use several anchors, not one at the centre
  • Relocalisation can fail if the room has changed: plan what the user sees when it does

Occlusion & Depth

  • Without occlusion, virtual objects always draw on top of the real world
  • Depth-based occlusion hides the parts of a virtual object that are behind real ones
  • Needs a per-frame depth map: the Quest 3 depth sensor, the ARCore Depth API, LiDAR on iPhone Pro models
  • In AR Foundation: AROcclusionManager
  • Wrong occlusion is one of the fastest ways to break Plausibility Illusion (Week 6)

Coordinate Spaces

Space Origin Notes
Tracking space Where tracking started Can shift on recentre or relocalisation
World / scene space The app’s chosen origin Usually the XR Origin in Unity
Anchor space A real place in the room Updated as tracking improves

Rule of thumb: parent placed content to an anchor, so it moves with the room when the device corrects its estimate.

The Privacy Question

A room scan is a detailed record of someone’s home: its layout, size, contents, even clues to where it is.

  • What exactly does your app store: planes, mesh, labels, camera images?
  • Does it stay on the device, or is it uploaded or shared?
  • Did you tell the user before asking for access, in plain language?

Platforms gate scene data behind a permission (on Quest, apps must request access to spatial data). The disclosure is still the designer’s job.

Key Takeaways

  • Spatial computing = awareness of the room + persistence of content in it
  • Tracking fuses cameras and IMU, and fails predictably: low light, blank or repetitive surfaces, covered cameras
  • Scene understanding gives planes, meshes and labels; some measured live, some declared in setup
  • Anchors are best estimates: keep content close to them and choose local, persistent or shared deliberately
  • Room data is personal data: be explicit about what you store and why
