Week 7 Lecture Notes

Spatial Mapping, Anchors & Scene Understanding

TipTL;DR
  • Spatial computing adds awareness and persistence to VR and overlay AR, delivered by three mechanisms: tracking, scene understanding and anchors.
  • Inside-out headsets fuse cameras and an IMU for 6DoF tracking, and use SLAM to build a map of the room while localising within it.
  • Tracking fails predictably in low light, on blank or repetitive surfaces and with covered cameras, so good apps show tracking state, guide the user and recover gracefully.
  • Scene understanding layers planes, meshes and semantic labels, measured live or declared in setup, enabling tap-to-place raycasting and depth-based occlusion of virtual content.
  • Anchors (local, persistent or shared) are best estimates that drift with distance: choose by persistence requirement, keep content close, and parent content to anchors, not world coordinates.
  • A room scan is a detailed record of someone’s home, so designers must say plainly what is stored, where it goes and what the user was told.

1 Overview

Duration: 1 hour

This week addresses the mechanics behind the word spatial in the module title. Where Weeks 1-6 treated the headset largely as a display and an input device, this session looks at how it builds and maintains a model of the room it is standing in, and how digital content can be given a durable place inside that model.

2 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  • Explain how a headset builds and maintains a map of a physical space
  • Predict the conditions under which tracking and relocalisation will fail, and design for them
  • Choose an anchoring strategy appropriate to a stated persistence requirement
  • Identify the privacy implications of continuous scene capture and state them plainly

3 1. What Makes Computing Spatial

Spatial computing adds two properties to VR and simple overlay AR:

  • Awareness: the device keeps a live model of the room around it: where the floor, walls and furniture are.
  • Persistence: digital content keeps its place in that room, as the user moves, when the app restarts, and on another day.

Three mechanisms deliver these properties, and the rest of this lecture takes them in turn:

Mechanism Question it answers
Tracking Where is the device in the room?
Scene understanding What is the room like?
Anchors Where does the content belong?

4 2. Tracking and Mapping

4.1 2.1 Inside-Out 6DoF Tracking

A tracked headset reports a pose many times a second: its position along three axes and its rotation about three axes (yaw, pitch and roll), hence six degrees of freedom (6DoF). Week 2 introduced the distinction between 3DoF and 6DoF devices; this week is about how the six are measured.

Modern standalone headsets and phones track inside-out: the sensors are on the device itself rather than mounted around the room (the outside-in approach of the Vive Pro’s base stations). Two kinds of sensor are combined:

  • Cameras observe visual features in the room. They give an accurate position but only once per camera frame, and they need light and texture.
  • An IMU (inertial measurement unit: accelerometer and gyroscope) measures acceleration and rotation hundreds of times a second. It is fast but, used alone, drifts within seconds because small errors accumulate.

Sensor fusion combines the two: the IMU fills in fast motion between frames and the cameras correct its drift. This combination is often called visual-inertial odometry.

4.2 2.2 SLAM in Outline

Tracking a device’s motion is only half the problem. To know where it is in a room, the device also needs a map of that room, and it has to build that map while using it. This is Simultaneous Localisation And Mapping (SLAM) (Durrant-Whyte & Bailey, 2006).

  1. Detect features: find distinctive, repeatable points in each camera image, such as corners, edges and textured patches.
  2. Estimate pose: match features between frames to work out how the device has moved.
  3. Update the map: add newly seen features to a 3D map of the space.
  4. Loop closure: when the device recognises a place it has seen before, it corrects the drift accumulated since then, and the whole map snaps into better alignment.
  5. Relocalisation: after tracking is lost, or when an app starts in a known room, the device matches what it sees against a saved map to recover its pose.

4.3 2.3 When Tracking Breaks

Because SLAM depends on seeing distinctive features, its failures are predictable:

Condition Why it fails What the user experiences
Low light Cameras see too few features Content drifts, then a “tracking lost” message
Blank walls, glossy or reflective surfaces No texture to match Content slides across surfaces
Repeating patterns (tiles, carpet, shelving) Different places look the same Pose jumps to the wrong place
Covered cameras, very fast motion Camera frames unusable View freezes, then snaps back
Moved furniture, a different room Saved map no longer matches Relocalisation fails; anchored content is missing

These failures are a design problem, not only an engineering one. Good spatial applications:

  • Show tracking state clearly, rather than letting content silently drift
  • Guide the user to look around slowly when the map is thin (most phone AR apps show a “move your phone” animation for this reason)
  • Recover gracefully (the Week 6 graceful degradation pattern): hide or fade content whose anchor cannot be found, rather than leaving it in the wrong place
  • Never freeze the view in a headset, which is a strong trigger for simulator sickness (Week 3)

5 3. Scene Understanding

Tracking tells the device where it is. Scene understanding tells it what is around it. The model of the room comes in layers:

  • Planes: flat, bounded surfaces such as the floor, walls, ceiling and table tops. Planes are cheap to compute and good for placing objects.
  • Meshes: a triangle surface over everything the device has seen, including irregular objects such as a sofa or a person. Meshes support physics (a virtual ball bouncing off a real chair) and occlusion.
  • Semantic labels: what a surface is: floor, wall, ceiling, table, couch, door, window. Labels let content behave sensibly: a virtual pet sits on the couch, a poster goes on a wall.

5.1 3.1 Measured vs Declared Geometry

There are two ways a device can know about a room, and they fail differently:

  • Measured geometry is detected live by the cameras and, on devices that have one, a depth sensor. It reflects the room as it is now, but it is incomplete until the user has looked around.
  • Declared geometry is captured once in a guided setup and stored. On Quest 3, Space Setup scans the room and lets the user confirm walls and furniture; apps then read this scene model. Declared geometry is complete and labelled from the first frame, but it goes stale when furniture moves.

When analysing or designing an experience, ask which kind it relies on, and what happens when the room no longer matches.

5.2 3.2 Plane Detection and Raycasting

The most common scene-understanding interaction is tap-to-place, which is this week’s lab. In Unity’s AR Foundation:

  1. An AR Session manages the tracking session, and an XR Origin holds the camera and the detected trackables (older tutorials call this rig AR Session Origin, which was replaced in AR Foundation 5).
  2. ARPlaneManager detects planes and adds a GameObject for each one.
  3. ARRaycastManager casts a ray from the screen tap (or a controller) against the detected planes and returns the hits.
  4. The app places a prefab at the hit pose, which carries rotation as well as position, so the object sits flat on the surface rather than at an arbitrary angle.

A placement indicator (a reticle that follows the detected surface) closes the Gulf of Evaluation from Week 6: the user sees where the object will appear before committing to it.

6 4. Spatial Anchors

An anchor is a pose that the device commits to tracking against the real world. Instead of placing content at fixed coordinates, the app attaches it to an anchor, and the device keeps the anchor aligned with the physical place as its understanding of the room improves.

6.1 4.1 Local, Persistent and Shared Anchors

Kind Survives Typical use
Local The current session only Objects placed during a single visit
Persistent App restarts and later days on the same device A virtual screen that stays on your wall
Shared Another user’s device in the same room Co-located multi-user experiences, such as two people looking at the same model

In AR Foundation, anchors are managed by ARAnchorManager. How persistence and sharing work depends on the platform: Meta’s spatial anchors can be saved on the headset and shared between users through Meta’s services; ARCore offers Cloud Anchors; ARKit can save and reload a world map. Cloud services also come and go: Microsoft retired Azure Spatial Anchors in 2024, and applications built on it had to be rebuilt. For a project, check what your target platform supports before designing around persistence.

6.2 4.2 Choosing an Anchoring Strategy

Start from the persistence requirement, not the technology:

  • Does the content only need to stay put while the user is looking at it? A local anchor is enough.
  • Must it be there tomorrow? You need a persistent anchor, plus a plan for when relocalisation fails (the room has changed, or the user is somewhere else).
  • Must two people see it in the same place? You need a shared anchor, and you inherit network, permission and privacy questions.
  • Should it follow the user instead? Then it should not be anchored to the room at all: use the body-locked UI from Week 5.

6.3 4.3 Anchor Drift and Its Limits

An anchor is the device’s best estimate of a real place, and that estimate changes as the map is refined. Two consequences matter for design:

  • Error grows with distance. A small error in an anchor’s rotation becomes a large error in position for content placed far from it, in the same way a slight change in angle moves the end of a long pole a long way.
  • Anchors can move. When the device corrects its map (for example after loop closure), anchored content shifts to match. This is the anchor working as intended, but a visible jump can still surprise the user.

Practical rules:

  • Place each anchor close to the content it holds, and give separate objects their own anchors
  • In large spaces, use several anchors rather than one at the centre
  • Do not create anchors every frame: each one has a tracking cost

7 5. Occlusion, Depth and Coordinate Spaces

7.1 5.1 Occlusion and Depth

By default, virtual content is rendered on top of the camera or passthrough image, so it always appears in front of real objects, even when it is meant to be behind them. Depth-based occlusion fixes this: the device estimates the distance to real surfaces in every frame, and hides the parts of virtual objects that lie behind them.

The depth map can come from a dedicated depth sensor (Quest 3, LiDAR on iPhone Pro models) or be estimated from camera motion (the ARCore Depth API). In AR Foundation it is exposed through AROcclusionManager. Depth maps are lower resolution than the camera image, so occlusion edges can look soft or flicker, especially around hands and hair.

Wrong occlusion is one of the fastest ways to break Plausibility Illusion (Week 6): an object that should be behind the sofa but floats in front of it no longer looks like it is in the room.

7.2 5.2 Coordinate Spaces

A spatial application juggles several coordinate spaces, each with its own origin:

Space Origin Notes
Tracking space Wherever tracking started Can shift when the user recentres or the device relocalises
World / scene space The application’s chosen origin In Unity, usually the XR Origin
Anchor space A real place in the room Updated as tracking improves
Local (object) space The object itself Position relative to its parent

The device converts between these spaces with transforms. The practical rule is simple: parent placed content to an anchor rather than placing it at fixed world coordinates. When the device corrects its estimate of the room, the anchor moves with the room and the content moves with the anchor. Content placed at fixed world coordinates stays where the old estimate put it, and visibly drifts away from the real surface.

8 6. The Privacy Question

A room scan is a detailed spatial record of someone’s home. It can reveal:

  • The layout and size of the home, and which room is the bedroom
  • Objects the user owns, and their value
  • Clues to location: the view from a window, the orientation of the room
  • When combined with camera images, people, documents and photographs in the room

Platforms gate this data. On Quest, for example, an app must request permission before it can read the user’s scene data. But a permission prompt tells the user very little about what this app will do with the data. A designer should be able to answer, plainly and before asking for access:

  1. What is stored? Planes and labels only, the full mesh, or camera images?
  2. Where does it go? Does it stay on the device, or is it uploaded, shared with other users, or used to train models?
  3. What did you tell the user? Was the purpose explained in plain language, at the moment it matters?

Collect only what the feature needs, keep it on the device where possible, and say so. De Guzman, Thilakarathna and Seneviratne (2019) survey these risks across mixed reality systems in more depth.

9 Summary

Spatial computing is distinguished from VR by persistence and awareness: content that knows where it is and stays there. Tracking and SLAM tell the device where it is, and fail predictably in low light, on blank or repetitive surfaces and when the cameras are covered. Scene understanding supplies planes, meshes and labels, some measured live and some declared in setup. Anchors are the mechanism for persistence; they are best estimates that drift with distance, so content should be kept close to them. And because a room scan is a record of a home, both scene understanding and anchors carry privacy obligations that are the designer’s to address.

10 Self-Check Questions

  1. Why does a headset need both cameras and an IMU to track its pose? What goes wrong with each on its own?
  2. Explain loop closure in one sentence. What does the user see when it happens?
  3. Name four conditions under which tracking degrades, and say what the user experiences in each.
  4. What is the difference between measured and declared scene geometry? Give one advantage of each.
  5. A museum wants a virtual exhibit label to appear beside the same painting every day. Which kind of anchor does it need, and what should happen if relocalisation fails?
  6. Why should content be parented to an anchor rather than placed at fixed world coordinates?
  7. List three things a room scan can reveal about a user, and three questions a designer should answer before asking for access to it.
