Performance, Comfort & Optimisation

Week 11 Overview

Performance is not polish applied at the end. A dropped frame is a physiological event for the person wearing the headset, so the frame budget is both a comfort requirement and an accessibility requirement. This session links back to the sensory conflict theory from Week 3 and gives students a method for finding the real bottleneck in their Assessment 3 build.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  • Relate frame timing directly to simulator sickness risk
  • Read a profiler capture and locate the actual bottleneck rather than the suspected one
  • Prioritise optimisations by measured cost rather than by intuition

These mirror the lecture notes. Return to them in the closing summary.

The Frame Budget Is a Comfort Requirement

  • Motion-to-photon latency: the time from a head movement to the matching pixels reaching the eye
  • Late or dropped frames make the world lag behind the head: the visual and vestibular senses disagree (sensory conflict, Week 3)
  • The result is discomfort, nausea and simulator sickness
  • Unlike a flat-screen game, a slow frame here is not just ugly: it is physically unpleasant

Revisit the sensory conflict theory from Week 3. The vestibular system reports that the head has moved; the display has not caught up. Ask students to recall their own SSQ scores from the Week 3 practical and whether any of the conditions had unstable frame rates.

Refresh Targets and Frame Time

Refresh rate Time per frame
72 Hz 13.9 ms
90 Hz 11.1 ms
120 Hz 8.3 ms
  • Everything the CPU and GPU do must finish inside this window, every frame
  • Missing it once is a dropped frame. Missing it repeatedly is a comfort failure.
  • A higher refresh rate is smoother and more comfortable, but gives you less time to do the work

Work through the arithmetic on the board: 1000 ms divided by the refresh rate. Stress the trade-off: choosing 120 Hz halves the budget compared with 60 Hz. The target is a design decision made at the start of a project, not at the end.

How a Mobile GPU Renders

  • Standalone headsets such as Quest use a Snapdragon XR2 chip: a tile-based mobile GPU
  • The screen is split into small tiles. Each tile is shaded in fast on-chip memory, then written out once.
  • This saves memory bandwidth, which is the scarce resource on mobile hardware
  • Anything that forces extra reads and writes of the whole screen works against the design

Keep this at the level students need for decisions. Tile-based rendering exists because memory bandwidth is expensive in power and heat on a battery-powered device in front of the user’s face. Note for the lecturer: the lecture notes say tile-based deferred rendering; Adreno GPUs are usually described as tile-based with a binning pass. Check the wording you prefer before presenting.

What Is Expensive on This Hardware

Cost Why it hurts on a tile-based GPU
Overdraw The same pixel is shaded several times by stacked surfaces
Transparency Cannot be depth-rejected early, so every layer is shaded
Full-screen effects Bloom, depth of field and similar force whole-screen passes through memory
Real-time shadows and lighting Extra passes and extra shading per pixel

Prefer opaque materials, baked lighting and simple shaders.

Overdraw is the classic culprit in XR scenes: particle systems, foliage cards, glass and UI panels. Ask students which of these exist in their Assessment 3 prototype. Full-screen post-processing that looks fine on a desktop GPU can halve the frame rate on a headset.

Draw Calls and Batching

  • A draw call is a request from the CPU to the GPU to draw a mesh with a material
  • Each call has CPU overhead, so many small objects can make you CPU-bound even when the GPU is idle
  • Material count matters: objects can only be combined if they share a material
Technique What it does Trade-off
Static batching Merges non-moving meshes that share a material Uses more memory
Dynamic batching Merges small moving meshes at runtime CPU cost to combine; only small meshes
GPU instancing Draws many copies of one mesh and material in one call Same mesh and material only

The relationship between material count and CPU cost is the key idea: fewer materials means more batching and fewer draw calls. Suggest texture atlases to let many objects share one material. If the project uses URP, mention the SRP Batcher as a related feature. Do not give a hard draw-call limit; tell students to measure on the device.

Reducing Draw Calls in Practice

  • Share materials: use one atlas texture for many props
  • Mark non-moving scenery as Static
  • Use GPU instancing for repeated objects (trees, pickups, bricks)
  • Remove objects the user can never see, and keep distant detail low
  • Check the result in the Frame Debugger: do the batches look the way you expected?

A short live demo works well here: open a scene with many separate materials, show the batch count, then merge materials and show it fall. Emphasise confirming that batching actually happened rather than assuming it did.

Foveated Rendering

  • The eye sees sharply only in a small central region (the fovea)
  • Foveated rendering shades the periphery at a lower resolution to save GPU time
  • Fixed foveated rendering: the high-quality region is fixed at the centre of the lens
  • Dynamic (eye-tracked) foveated rendering: the high-quality region follows the gaze. It needs eye tracking hardware.

Fixed foveated rendering assumes the user looks through the middle of the lens. Eye-tracked foveation can be more aggressive because it knows where the user is looking. Link back to the eye tracking material in Week 4.

Foveated Rendering: What It Costs

Fixed Eye-tracked
Saving Moderate, always on Larger, because the sharp area is smaller
Needs Any supported headset Eye tracking hardware and user permission
Artefact Blocky or shimmering edges in the periphery, visible when looking off-centre Visible if tracking lags behind a fast eye movement
  • Stronger settings save more and show more artefacts: test on device, with real users
  • Text, UI and fine detail near the edge suffer first

Every optimisation buys performance with a visible or perceptual cost. Ask students to try the different foveation levels on a headset and decide where it becomes noticeable. Remind them that eye tracking data is sensitive and requires consent (Week 5 ethics material).

Application SpaceWarp

  • Application SpaceWarp (ASW) lets the app render at half the display rate
  • The system synthesises the in-between frames from the app’s motion vectors and depth
  • Result: roughly twice the time per rendered frame, while the headset still refreshes at full rate
  • Needs the app to supply motion vectors and depth buffers correctly

This is the biggest single performance trade available on Quest. The app might render at 36 fps while the headset shows 72. Make clear it is a synthesis, not magic: the system is guessing what the missing frames look like.

SpaceWarp: What It Costs

  • Ghosting and smearing around fast-moving objects
  • Warping at edges, where the system has to guess what was hidden behind a moving object
  • Poor results with transparency, particles and anything without correct motion vectors
  • Adds a little latency, and can hide a problem rather than fix it

Use it as a last resort for the frame budget, not as a substitute for profiling.

A quick comparison clip, with and without ASW on a fast-moving object, makes the artefacts concrete. Raise the comfort question: an artefact that is mildly distracting on a screen can be more noticeable and more uncomfortable in a headset.

A Profiling Methodology

  1. Set a target: refresh rate and frame time
  2. Measure a baseline on the headset, not in the editor
  3. Find the bottleneck: CPU-bound or GPU-bound?
  4. Change one thing
  5. Measure again and compare with the baseline
  6. Keep it if it helped. Revert it if it did not.
  • Changing several things at once means you cannot tell which one worked
  • Write down each change and its measured effect
  • Editor performance is not device performance: the desktop GPU hides problems

The discipline is the point. Students often optimise what they suspect rather than what they measured. Ask for a show of hands: who has already optimised something without measuring it first? Encourage a simple optimisation log in the project repository.

The Tools

Tool What it shows Use it to
Unity Profiler CPU and GPU time per frame, broken down by system Find which scripts or systems consume the frame
Frame Debugger Each draw call and batch in order Check batching and spot overdraw sources
OVR Metrics Tool On-device frame rate, stale frames, CPU and GPU utilisation Measure the real headset, without the editor

Use the Unity Profiler and OVR Metrics together: one explains the cost, the other confirms it on the device.

Demonstrate attaching the Unity Profiler to a running Quest build over a cable or wireless connection, and enabling the OVR Metrics overlay. Show how to tell a CPU-bound frame from a GPU-bound one.

Reading a Capture

  • Look at the longest frames, not the average: comfort is lost in the spikes
  • Is the CPU or the GPU the longer bar? Fix the longer one first.
  • CPU-bound? Look at draw calls, scripts and physics
  • GPU-bound? Look at overdraw, transparency, resolution and effects
  • The cause is often not the one you suspected

Work through one capture as a class if possible. Common surprises: a script allocating memory every frame and triggering the garbage collector, a single particle system causing most of the GPU load, or physics running on far too many objects.

Sometimes the Answer Is to Cut Scope

  • If you are still over budget after the cheap fixes, reduce what the scene asks for
  • Fewer objects, simpler materials, a smaller play space, fewer simultaneous effects
  • A simpler experience that holds frame rate is better than a rich one that makes people feel unwell
  • This is a design decision and an accessibility decision, not a failure

For Assessment 3: depth over breadth also protects your frame rate.

Connect this to the scope advice in the Assessment 3 brief. A polished vertical slice that holds its frame rate will be rated more comfortable and more usable than a bigger one that stutters. Remind students that SSQ and SUS results from their testing will reflect performance problems.

Key Takeaways

  • The frame budget is a comfort requirement: late frames cause sensory conflict and simulator sickness
  • On a tile-based mobile GPU, overdraw, transparency and full-screen effects are the costly items
  • Batching and instancing cut draw calls. Foveation and SpaceWarp buy GPU time at the cost of artefacts.
  • Measure, change one thing, measure again, using the Unity Profiler and OVR Metrics
  • Be willing to cut content rather than ship something uncomfortable

Discussion

  • Is a stable 72 Hz with simple visuals better than a richer scene at an unstable frame rate? Who decides, and who is affected most?
  • Application SpaceWarp trades artefacts for frame rate. When is that trade acceptable, and when is it not?
  • Eye-tracked foveated rendering needs gaze data. What consent and privacy questions does that raise?
  • Think of a time you optimised something by instinct. What would have changed if you had measured first?
  • Which part of your Assessment 3 prototype is most likely to blow its frame budget, and what would you cut first?

Use these to close the lecture or as a forum prompt. The last question is a bridge to the lab: students should predict their own bottleneck before they profile it, then compare the prediction with the capture.
