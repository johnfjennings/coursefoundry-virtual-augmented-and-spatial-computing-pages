Film and screen games control attention with the camera. In XR the user holds the camera, so attention has to be invited by the environment itself. This week is about the tools that do that, and why each is also a comfort and performance decision.

The User Owns the Viewpoint

Film and conventional games direct attention with the camera:

  • Cuts and close-ups choose what you see
  • Camera moves carry you through a space

In XR these are unavailable or harmful: a forced camera move causes sickness, and a cut breaks presence.

What replaces them:

  • Composition — what is placed where
  • Light — where the eye is drawn
  • Form — what looks usable, and where to go
  • Sound and motion — what turns the head (Week 10)

Design at 1:1

In XR the user’s body is the ruler. Everyone knows how big a door, a table or a step should be.

Object Typical real size
Standing eye height ~1.5–1.7 m
Door ~2.0 m high, 0.8–0.9 m wide
Table / desk ~0.75 m
Kitchen counter ~0.9 m
Ceiling ~2.4–2.7 m

Games often scale spaces up for a third-person camera. In a headset those spaces feel empty and wrong. 1 Unity unit = 1 metre.

When Scale Goes Wrong

Wrong scale changes how users perceive themselves:

  • Too big: users feel small, even child-like
  • Too small: users feel like giants in a model

Common causes

  • Assets modelled at game or arbitrary scale
  • Camera height set in code instead of from tracking
  • A virtual floor that does not match the real floor

Check scale from inside: stand next to it, reach for it, sit at it. Scale errors are nearly invisible in the editor and obvious in the headset.

Comfort Zones for Content

Zone Distance Use it for
Reach ~0.5–0.7 m Things to touch and grab
Mid field ~1–3 m UI, text, focal content
Far field Beyond ~10 m Scenery, landmarks
  • Comfortable head rotation is about ±30° left and right, ±55° at most (Alger, 2015)
  • Looking down is easier than looking up
  • Keep important content in front; place secondary content to the sides, never only behind

Sightlines

A sightline is a view the user is likely to take: on arrival, at a turn, through a doorway.

  • Frame the important thing: doorways, arches, gaps between objects
  • Align it with the direction of travel
  • Light it so it stands out from its surroundings
  • Leave negative space around it, so it is not lost in clutter

Plan sightlines on the floor plan, then walk them in the headset.

Wayfinding

Kevin Lynch (1960) found people navigate cities using five elements, and they work in virtual spaces too:

Element In an XR environment
Paths Corridors, trails, lit routes
Edges Walls, water, cliffs, play-area limits
Districts Areas with a distinct look or sound
Nodes Junctions and rooms where choices are made
Landmarks Tall, unique, lit objects visible from far away

Signs and arrows are a last resort: they are UI, and they break presence.

Affordance Through Form

Signifiers, built into the world instead of into a UI:

  • Shape — handles say pull, raised buttons say press, edges say stop
  • Consistency — one visual signal (a rim, a glow, a material) for everything interactive, and never on scenery
  • Wear — scuffs, worn paths and fingerprints show where people have been
  • Light — a pool of light says come here

Test it: can a first-time user tell what to touch without being told?

Lighting as Direction

Light is the closest thing XR has to a camera.

  • The eye goes to the brightest area and to contrast
  • Warm against cool and motion against stillness pull attention too
  • A lit doorway or a pool of light is an invitation
  • Keep the rest of the scene readable: too dark and users cannot find their way, or their footing

If the user can look anywhere, the light decides where they look first.

Lighting on a Mobile GPU Budget

A standalone headset renders two eyes at 72–120 Hz on a mobile GPU: about 11 ms per frame at 90 Hz.

Technique Cost Use for
Baked lightmaps Very low at runtime Static walls, floors, furniture
Light probes Low Lighting moving objects in a baked scene
Reflection probes Low (baked) Shiny surfaces
Realtime lights High, especially with shadows One key light at most

Bake what does not move. This week’s lab does exactly this.

Environmental Storytelling

The arrangement of a space can tell a story with no text at all.

  • Embedded narrative (Jenkins, 2004): the story happened before the user arrived, and they piece it together by looking
  • Clues work best at sightline points and within the mid field
  • Change the space to show state: a door now open, a light now on, an object moved

Environment Decisions Are Comfort Decisions

Problem Why Instead
Low ceilings Claustrophobia 2.4 m or more, unless intended
High-contrast moving patterns Vection: illusory self-motion Calm textures on large surfaces
Unprotected heights Fear, real stumbles Railings, or keep users back
Stairs with smooth locomotion Repeated vertical bumps Ramps, or teleport
Virtual floor ≠ real floor Wrong scale, falls Floor-level tracking origin
Small distant text Unreadable Size text for its distance

Key Takeaways

  • In XR the environment is the interface: attention is invited by composition, light, form and sound
  • Model at 1:1 and check scale in the headset, not the editor
  • Put content where people can comfortably look and reach
  • Guide with sightlines, landmarks and light before reaching for signs
  • Every environment decision is also a comfort and a performance decision

Discussion

  • When is it right to break 1:1 scale on purpose? Give an example where a deliberately wrong scale is the point.
  • Signs and arrows are UI. Are they always a design failure in XR, or are there spaces where they are the honest choice?
  • Light draws the eye, but some users have low vision or light sensitivity. How would you guide them through the same space?
  • How much environmental storytelling can a user miss before the experience stops making sense?
  • Baking lighting makes a scene fast but static. What kinds of experiences cannot be baked, and how would you light them on a standalone headset?

Use these to connect the session to the greybox practical: students should leave able to name the sightlines, landmarks and comfort zones in their own Assessment 3 environment.
