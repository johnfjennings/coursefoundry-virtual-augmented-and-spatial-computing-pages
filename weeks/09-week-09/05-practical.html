Week 9 Practical (Optional)

Capturing Reality with Gaussian Splats

NoteTL;DR

Capture a real object and a room corner as Gaussian splats, clean and reduce them, view them in the headset, and measure what they gain (presence, visual fidelity) against what they cost (artefacts, frame time, no interactivity). Finish with an evidence-based recommendation for how your Assessment 3 environment should be represented. The Part 5 extension imports your splat into a Unity project and views it on a Quest 2.

Overview

Duration: 2 hours, plus about 45 minutes for the optional Unity extension in Part 5

Gaussian splatting reconstructs a real place from photos or video as millions of small, semi-transparent, view-dependent blobs. The result can look photoreal in a way a mesh rarely does, but a splat has no surfaces, no collision and no relightable materials, and it is expensive to draw. This optional practical replaces the hype with measurement: you capture a real space, put it in the headset, and test whether the fidelity is worth its cost for your own project.

It builds directly on this week’s greybox: the blockout you made in the main practical is the baseline you compare the splat against.

Objectives

By the end of this practical, you will be able to:

  • Plan and execute a capture that a splat reconstruction can actually use
  • Clean and reduce a splat, and explain what each reduction trades away
  • Measure the rendering cost of a splat against the frame budget from Week 11
  • Judge when a splat is the right representation for a world, and when a mesh or a greybox is
  • Import a splat into a Unity project and view it on a Quest 2 (Part 5 extension)

Prerequisites

  • A phone with a splat-capable capture app (for example Scaniverse or Polycam), or access to a lab capture device
  • A computer with a browser for editing (SuperSplat runs in the browser)
  • A Quest 3 or Quest 2, and either a browser-based WebXR splat viewer or a PC VR setup over Link
  • Your Week 9 greybox, and the four presence items from the Week 10 practical
  • For Part 5: your Unity 6 project from the Week 4 practical (URP, OpenXR and an XR Origin), a Quest 2 with a Link cable or Air Link, and a Windows PC with a dedicated graphics card
WarningCheck the toolchain first

Splat tooling changes quickly. App names, export formats (.ply, .spz, .sog), Unity package versions and headset support move from term to term. Confirm the capture app, the editor, the viewer and the Unity import route on a lab headset before the session, and use whichever current tool gives you a splat file you can edit and view.

Instructions

Part 1: Capture (35 minutes)

  1. Choose two subjects: one object on a table (about 30 cm across) and one corner of a room (about 3 × 3 m).
  2. Capture each twice.
    • A careful pass: move slowly, orbit at three heights, and overlap every view with the last.
    • A deliberately bad pass: move quickly, from one height, and leave gaps in coverage.
  3. Include the hard cases in the room corner: a mirror or window, something thin (a plant or chair legs) and a plain, untextured wall. These are the same failure conditions as the Week 7 tracking practical, now tested against a different reconstruction method.
  4. Optional: capture the same room with the Quest 3’s own room-capture feature, if it is available on the lab headsets, and compare the two.

Part 2: Clean and Reduce (30 minutes)

  1. Open the careful room capture in SuperSplat. Crop away everything outside the room and delete floating fragments (“floaters”).
  2. Record the splat count and file size.
  3. Export three versions: full, about 50 % of the splats, and about 20 %.
  4. For each reduction, note what disappears first: fine detail, reflections, or the edges of the scene.

Part 3: Into the Headset (35 minutes)

  1. View all three versions in the headset, either standalone in a WebXR splat viewer or as PC VR through Unity over Link (Part 5 walks through the Unity route on a Quest 2). Desktop splat renderers for Unity generally target PC GPUs rather than standalone Quest. That limit is part of what you are investigating.
  2. Stand where the camera stood, then walk to places it never went. Record where the illusion breaks: blur up close, smearing at unseen angles, floaters.
  3. Measure frame rate or frame time for each version using the method from the Week 11 practical. Find the largest version that holds the headset’s refresh rate.
  4. Try to touch the table. Your hand passes through it, because a splat has no collider. Write down what you would have to add to make the space interactive.

Part 4: Compare and Recommend (20 minutes)

  1. With a partner administering, rate presence for three versions of the same corner: the full splat, the 20 % splat, and your greybox. Use the four presence items from Week 10 and randomise the order.
  2. Write a recommendation of no more than 300 words for your Assessment 3 environment: splat, mesh, greybox, or a hybrid (for example, a splat backdrop with simple invisible colliders). Justify it with your own frame-time and presence data.

Part 5 (Extension): A Splat in Unity on a Quest 2 (45 minutes)

The Quest 2 is the headset many of you will build for, and it is the hardest test for a splat: an older first-generation Snapdragon XR2, less memory than a Quest 3, and no colour passthrough. Splats need a GPU renderer, and Unity has none built in. This part uses Unity Gaussian Splatting, an open-source (MIT-licensed) renderer by Aras Pranckevičius. There are two routes to the headset:

  • PC VR over Link (main route). Unity renders the splat on the PC’s graphics card and streams the picture to the Quest 2. This is the route that reliably works.
  • A standalone Android build (stretch). The splat renders on the Quest 2’s own GPU. The renderer targets desktop GPUs, so expect this to be slow or not to run at all. Either result is a valid finding.

5.1 Set Up the Project

  1. Open your Unity 6 project from Week 4. It already has URP, OpenXR and an XR Origin.

  2. Install the renderer. Open Window > Package Manager, click +, choose Install package from git URL and enter:

    https://github.com/aras-p/UnityGaussianSplatting.git?path=package

  3. Add the URP renderer feature. Find the Universal Renderer Data asset your project uses (it is listed in Project Settings > Quality under the URP asset for each level, usually PC_Renderer and Mobile_Renderer in the Settings folder). Select it, click Add Renderer Feature and choose Gaussian Splat URP Feature. Without it, nothing is drawn.

  4. Set the Windows graphics API. In Project Settings > Player, open the Windows, Mac, Linux tab and expand Other Settings > Graphics APIs for Windows. Untick Auto Graphics API, then add Direct3D12 and drag it to the top. The renderer needs compute shaders and does not support Direct3D 11. Restart the editor when Unity asks you to.

WarningDirect3D 12 and the Week 4 simulator tip

Week 4 suggested forcing Direct3D 11 to keep the Meta XR Simulator stable. The splat renderer needs Direct3D 12 (or Vulkan), so the two settings conflict. Use a copy of your project for this part, or switch the API back when you return to the simulator. If the headset shows a black view over Link with Direct3D 12, try Vulkan as the first API instead.

5.2 Import the Splat

  1. In SuperSplat, export the three versions from Part 2 (full, ~50 % and ~20 %) as .ply files. The importer reads PLY files, and some versions also read .spz.
  2. In Unity, open Tools > Gaussian Splats > Create GaussianSplatAsset.
  3. Set Input PLY/SPZ File to your full export, choose the Quality level (start with Medium; lower levels compress harder and produce smaller assets), set the output folder to Assets/Splats, and click Create Asset.
  4. Repeat for the 50 % and 20 % exports. Record the asset size for each one alongside the file sizes from Part 2.

5.3 Place It in the Scene

  1. Create an empty GameObject called Room Splat, add a Gaussian Splat Renderer component and drag the full asset into its Asset field.
  2. Fix the orientation. Captures often arrive upside down or tilted. Rotate the GameObject (an X rotation of 180° is a common fix) until the floor is level.
  3. Fix the scale and floor height. Splats are rarely at true scale. Place a 1 m reference cube on the table you captured and scale Room Splat uniformly until they match. Then move it so the captured floor sits at Y = 0, the same as your XR Origin’s floor. Do this in the headset, not on the monitor, for the same reason the greybox is judged in the headset.
  4. Make it a hybrid. Add invisible Box Colliders over the floor and the table, and a Teleportation Area on the floor collider. The splat provides the look; the colliders provide the physics. This is the hybrid from Part 4.
  5. Optional: add a Gaussian Cutout component to hide parts of the splat (a doorway, a person who walked through the capture) without re-editing the file.

5.4 View It on the Quest 2 over Link

  1. Connect the Quest 2 by Link cable or Air Link, open the Meta Horizon Link app on the PC, and make sure it is the active OpenXR runtime (in the app’s settings). If you used the Meta XR Simulator in Week 4, switch the simulator off first: only one OpenXR runtime can be active at a time.
  2. In Project Settings > XR Plug-in Management, on the Windows, Mac, Linux tab, tick OpenXR and add the Oculus Touch Controller Profile.
  3. Press Play and put on the headset. Swap the three assets into Room Splat in turn.
  4. Measure each version with the Unity Profiler and record the frame time against the Quest 2’s budget (13.9 ms at its default 72 Hz). Over Link the cost lands on the PC’s graphics card, so record which card it was: the same splat on a weaker PC may not hold the frame rate.

5.5 Stretch: A Standalone Quest 2 Build

  1. Switch the build profile to Meta Quest (Android). In Project Settings > Player > Android > Other Settings, untick Auto Graphics API and leave Vulkan as the only API: the renderer needs compute shaders.
  2. Use the 20 % asset at a low quality level. It is the only one with a realistic chance on the Quest 2.
  3. Build And Run, then record: does it render at all, how long does it take to load, what frame rate does the OVR Metrics overlay report, and how does it look compared with the same file in the WebXR viewer?
  4. Expect a frame rate well below 72 Hz, or no image at all. Record what happened. It is evidence for the recommendation in Part 4: on a standalone Quest 2, a mesh or a greybox may be the only representation that holds the frame budget.
NoteReady-made scene

If your lecturer has shared the course’s Unity demo project, its Week09_GaussianSplats scene already has the renderer set up, with slots for full, 50 % and 20 % assets beside a greybox corner, a frame-time readout and the presence questions. Use it to check your results against your own project.

Expected Outcome

  • Two captures of each subject (careful and bad), with the differences annotated
  • A table of splat count, file size and frame time for the three reduction levels
  • A list of at least four distinct splat failures, each tied to the capture condition that caused it
  • Presence ratings for the full splat, the reduced splat and the greybox
  • A recommendation of no more than 300 words for your own project, supported by that evidence
  • If you completed Part 5: your splat placed at true scale in a Unity scene and viewed on a Quest 2 over Link, a frame-time table for the three versions with the PC’s graphics card noted, and a note on whether the standalone Quest 2 build ran
TipHint

A splat is a recording of light from the places the camera went. Every artefact you find marks a position or an angle the capture never covered, so plan the capture path the way you planned sightlines in the greybox.

ImportantCapturing real spaces

A splat records everything the camera saw, including people, screens and documents. Capture only spaces you have permission to capture, keep people out of frame, and check what is visible before you share a file. This ties into the ethics discussion in Week 13.
