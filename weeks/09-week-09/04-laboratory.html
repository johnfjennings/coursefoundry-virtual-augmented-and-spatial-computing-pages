Week 9 Lab

Lighting and Environment Production

1 Lab Overview

Duration: 2 hours Hardware: Meta Quest 3 / 3S (Quest 2 acceptable) Software: Unity 2022 LTS (URP), XR Interaction Toolkit 2.x Starting scene: your greybox from this week’s practical. If you do not have one yet, build a simple room (floor, four walls, a ceiling, a table and a few boxes) at true scale. Deliverable: Technical note (150–250 words) with before-and-after measurements, for your Assessment 1 portfolio

2 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lab you will be able to:

  • Bake lighting appropriate to a standalone headset’s performance budget
  • Light moving objects in a baked scene with light probes
  • Configure LOD groups, occlusion culling, reflection probes and a skybox for a mobile target
  • Use light and contrast to direct attention to a focal object, and confirm it on device
  • Measure performance on the headset rather than in the editor

3 Part 1: Lightmap Baking (45 min)

3.1 Task 1.1 — Take a Baseline

Before changing anything, record a baseline so you can show what your changes achieved:

  1. In the editor, open the Stats panel in the Game view and note Batches and Tris.
  2. Build to the headset as a Development Build with Autoconnect Profiler ticked (File > Build Settings), and note the frame time in the Unity Profiler (Window > Analysis > Profiler).

The editor’s Stats panel is fine for counting batches and triangles, but its frame time is your PC’s, not the headset’s. Always measure frame time on the device.

3.2 Task 1.2 — Prepare the Geometry

  1. Select the walls, floor, ceiling and furniture: everything that will never move.
  2. In the Inspector’s Static drop-down, tick Contribute GI. (Ticking Static sets all flags, which you also need for Part 2.)
  3. For any imported model, open its import settings and tick Generate Lightmap UVs, then Apply. Without lightmap UVs, a bake produces smeared or black surfaces. Unity’s built-in primitives already have them.

3.3 Task 1.3 — Set Up the Lights

  1. Set each light’s Mode to Baked. If you want one light to cast realtime shadows from moving objects, set only that one to Mixed.
  2. Keep the number of lights small. A key light, a fill light and one accent light are usually enough.

3.4 Task 1.4 — Bake

  1. Open Window > Rendering > Lighting.
  2. Under Lightmapping Settings, choose Progressive GPU. If your PC’s graphics card is not supported, Unity falls back to Progressive CPU, which is slower but gives the same result.
  3. Start with a Lightmap Resolution of about 10–20 texels per unit and a Max Lightmap Size of 1024 or 2048: enough for a headset, small enough to bake quickly.
  4. Click Generate Lighting, then inspect the result in the Baked Lightmaps tab. Look for waste: large lightmap areas spent on surfaces the user will never see closely.

3.5 Task 1.5 — Light Moving Objects

Baked lighting does not reach objects that move, so a grabbable object will look flat and unlit against baked walls.

  1. Add GameObject > Light > Light Probe Group.
  2. Place probes through the areas where moving objects and hands will be: around the table, along paths, near light and shadow boundaries. Keep them about a metre or two apart where the lighting changes, sparser elsewhere.
  3. Bake again, then move a non-static object through the scene in Play mode: it should pick up the light and shade of where it is.

4 Part 2: LODs and Culling (30 min)

4.1 Task 2.1 — LOD Groups

For any detailed model in your scene (an Asset Store prop, or a high-poly sphere or cylinder as a stand-in):

  1. Add an LOD Group component.
  2. Assign the detailed mesh to LOD 0 and a simpler version to LOD 1, and set Culled for very small screen sizes.
  3. Move the scene camera away and watch the LOD switch.

Greybox primitives are already simple and do not need LODs. Spend your time on whatever is most detailed in your scene.

4.2 Task 2.2 — Occlusion Culling

  1. Make sure large walls and solid objects are marked Occluder Static, and smaller objects Occludee Static.
  2. Open Window > Rendering > Occlusion Culling and click Bake.
  3. Use the Visualization tab to check that objects behind walls are culled from the camera’s view.

Occlusion culling helps most in spaces divided into rooms. In a single open room, expect little change.

4.3 Task 2.3 — Measure

Repeat the measurements from Task 1.1: batches and triangles in the editor, and frame time on the headset. Record the before-and-after numbers in a table for your technical note.

5 Part 3: Atmosphere and Attention (45 min)

5.1 Task 3.1 — Skybox and Reflections

  1. Set a Skybox Material under Window > Rendering > Lighting > Environment. Simple procedural skies are cheap.
  2. Add GameObject > Light > Reflection Probe, set its Type to Baked, size its box to the room, and bake. Shiny surfaces now reflect the room rather than a default sky.

5.2 Task 3.2 — Direct Attention With Light

Choose one focal object: the thing in your scene the user must notice first, ideally the object of your Assessment 3 core interaction. Without adding any UI:

  • Make it the brightest area in its part of the scene: a pool of light on it, a darker surround
  • Use colour contrast: a warm accent against cooler surroundings, or the reverse
  • Frame it with the sightline from the user’s start position (Week 9 lecture)

Bake again after each change.

5.3 Task 3.3 — Test on Device

Deploy to the headset. Ask someone who has not seen your scene to put it on, and time how long it takes them to point at the focal object. If it takes more than three seconds, adjust the lighting and try again with someone else.

6 Checkpoint

A lit, culled environment running on device at a steady frame rate, with the focal object identifiable within three seconds by someone who has not seen the scene before.

7 Deliverable

Add to your Assessment 1 portfolio:

  • A screenshot from the headset showing the lit scene and the focal object
  • A technical note (150–250 words) covering what you baked, the before-and-after numbers from Tasks 1.1 and 2.3, and how long your tester took to find the focal object
