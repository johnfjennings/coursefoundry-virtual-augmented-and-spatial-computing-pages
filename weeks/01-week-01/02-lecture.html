Introduction to Spatial Computing

1 Lecture Notes

2 Introduction

This module sits at the intersection of technology, design, and human experience. By the time you finish it, you should be able to think critically about immersive systems, build functional XR prototypes, and evaluate them against established principles of human-computer interaction.

This first week establishes the conceptual landscape. Before we can design or build anything, we need a shared vocabulary and a clear understanding of what XR actually is, where it came from, and where it is going.

It is worth being honest about why the vocabulary matters, because “getting the definitions right” can look like an academic exercise. It is not. The terms in this lecture map directly onto engineering decisions. Whether a system is optical see-through or video see-through determines what your content can look like, because you cannot render black on an optical display. Whether tracking is 3DOF or 6DOF determines whether a user can lean around an object, which in turn determines whether your scene can contain anything worth leaning around. Whether a device understands the room it is in determines whether your content can sit on a real table or must float in front of the user’s face. Every one of these is a design constraint before it is a technical specification, and the words we use are how we reason about them.

A second point before we begin. This field has an unusually strong marketing vocabulary running alongside its technical one, and the two frequently disagree. Manufacturers describe their products in whatever terms are currently favourable — the same headset has been sold as VR, as mixed reality, and as spatial computing within a few years, without the hardware changing much. Part of your job on this module is to be able to look past the positioning and describe what a device actually does. The hype cycle section below gives you a framework for doing exactly that.

3 Defining the XR Spectrum

The term XR (Extended Reality) is an umbrella that covers several related but distinct technologies. Understanding the differences between them is essential because each imposes different design constraints and affords different possibilities.

3.1 Virtual Reality (VR)

In a VR system, the user’s visual field is entirely replaced by a synthetic environment. The headset blocks out the physical world and presents a stereoscopic 3D image that responds to the user’s head movement. High-quality VR systems also include spatial audio, hand controllers with haptic feedback, and room-scale tracking.

The defining characteristic of VR is sensory substitution — the system replaces real sensory input with synthetic input. The quality of this substitution determines how convincing the experience feels.

Two consequences follow from total visual replacement, and they shape almost everything in the rest of this module.

The first is that the system becomes responsible for the user’s safety. Someone in a VR headset cannot see the floor, the furniture, other people, or their own hands unless you draw them. This is why guardian and boundary systems exist, and why every lab session in this module begins by clearing a floor area. It is also why a VR experience that encourages large physical movement carries a duty of care that a screen-based one does not.

The second is that the system becomes responsible for the user’s comfort in a way no screen is. If the image lags behind head movement, the user’s visual system reports one thing and their vestibular system reports another, and the result is nausea. We return to this properly in Week 3, but note now that this is a physiological response to a technical failure — not a matter of the user being sensitive or insufficiently accustomed.

3.2 Augmented Reality (AR)

AR systems overlay digital content onto the user’s view of the real world. This can be achieved through:

  • optical see-through displays (the user looks through a transparent lens with digital content projected onto it — HoloLens 2, Snap Spectacles)
  • video see-through displays (cameras capture the real world and the combined image is displayed — Quest 3 passthrough, mobile AR)
  • projection-based AR (digital content is projected directly onto real surfaces)

The defining characteristic of AR is that the real world remains visible and the digital content must be registered accurately to physical space.

That word registered is doing a great deal of work. Registration means the digital content holds its correct position relative to the physical world as the user moves. A virtual object that slides a few centimetres when you turn your head is not augmenting reality; it is floating in front of your eyes. Registration quality is the single clearest technical difference between a convincing AR system and an unconvincing one, and it is why Week 7 spends an entire session on spatial mapping and anchors.

The distinction between optical and video see-through has consequences that catch people out repeatedly, so it is worth stating plainly:

  • You cannot render black on an optical see-through display. These displays add light to the scene; they cannot subtract it. Black is simply the absence of projected light, which means it is transparent. Dark UI, drop shadows and solid backgrounds all fail. Content must be designed to read as luminous.
  • Video see-through can render black, but everything you see is a camera image. Real-world colour, dynamic range and latency are all mediated by the camera pipeline. The real world is subject to the same frame budget as your content.
  • Occlusion works differently in each. On a video see-through system, you can composite virtual objects behind real ones if you have depth data. On most optical systems, virtual content is additive light and will appear to ghost through real objects regardless.

3.3 Mixed Reality (MR)

Mixed reality is a term used in different ways by different organisations. In its most precise sense (following Milgram and Kishino’s taxonomy), MR refers to any point on the reality-virtuality continuum between fully real and fully virtual. In practice, it is often used to describe systems where digital and physical objects interact — for example, a virtual ball that bounces off a real table.

The HoloLens 2 and Meta Quest 3 are both marketed as mixed reality devices.

The looseness of this term is worth naming rather than resolving. In current industry usage, “mixed reality” most often signals that a headset can show you the real world through passthrough and place content in it with some awareness of geometry. That is a meaningful capability, but it is a narrower claim than Milgram’s, and when you read a specification sheet you should establish which sense is meant. The practical test is to ask what the system knows about the room: a device that merely shows you a camera feed is doing something quite different from one that has detected your walls, floor and furniture and can place a virtual object on your actual desk.

3.4 Spatial Computing

Spatial computing is a broader paradigm that describes computing systems that understand and interact with three-dimensional space. Rather than confining computation to a screen, spatial computing embeds it in the environment. This includes XR devices but also extends to robotics, autonomous vehicles, and smart environments.

Apple’s Vision Pro is positioned explicitly as a spatial computing device rather than a VR or AR headset, reflecting a deliberate shift in how the industry frames these technologies.

The framing shift is not only marketing. It carries a real claim: that the interesting property of these systems is not the display but the spatial awareness — the device’s model of where it is, what is around it, and how the user relates to both. On that reading, a headset is one form factor among several, and the defining capability is persistence. Content that knows where it is, and is still there tomorrow, behaves like part of the environment rather than like an application you launched.

This is the sense in which the module title uses the term, and it is why the second half of the semester moves from interaction technique into mapping, anchoring and environment design.

4 The Reality-Virtuality Continuum

Milgram and Kishino (1994) proposed a taxonomy of mixed reality visual displays that remains influential today. They described a continuum from fully real environments to fully virtual environments, with augmented reality and augmented virtuality occupying the middle ground.

Real Environment Augmented Reality Augmented Virtuality Virtual Environment
Fully real Mostly real, some virtual Mostly virtual, some real Fully virtual

This framework is useful because it reminds us that the distinction between VR and AR is not binary. Many modern devices can operate at different points on this continuum depending on their mode of use.

Augmented virtuality is the least familiar of these terms and the most useful for understanding modern hardware. It describes a predominantly virtual environment into which real elements are imported — a virtual meeting room containing a live video feed of a real colleague, or a fully synthetic scene in which your real hands are visible. A Quest 3 running a VR application that shows your real keyboard through passthrough is operating in augmented virtuality, and this is now a common design pattern rather than an exotic one.

Two cautions about applying the taxonomy.

First, it is a taxonomy of visual displays, published in 1994 and framed accordingly. It says nothing about audio, haptics, interaction technique or social presence, all of which matter as much as the visual channel and some of which we will argue matter more. When you position an experience on the continuum in Week 6, you are describing one dimension of it.

Second, modern devices move along the continuum at runtime. A single Quest 3 application may open in passthrough, fade to a fully virtual environment for a task, and return. The useful question is therefore not “where does this device sit” but “where does this experience sit, at this moment, and why did the designer choose that point?” That question is the basis of the analysis you will produce for Assessment 2.

5 A Brief History of XR

Understanding the history of XR helps contextualise the current moment and avoid repeating past mistakes.

5.1 Early Foundations (1960s–1990s)

  • 1965: Ivan Sutherland describes the “Ultimate Display” — a room in which the computer can control the existence of matter
  • 1968: Sutherland builds the first HMD, the Sword of Damocles
  • 1987: Jaron Lanier coins the term “virtual reality”
  • 1992: The CAVE (Cave Automatic Virtual Environment) is developed at the University of Illinois
  • 1994: Milgram and Kishino publish their taxonomy of mixed reality

Sutherland’s 1965 essay is worth reading in full — it is three pages long and remarkably undated. His formulation of the goal, a display in which matter itself can be controlled by the computer, still describes the ambition better than most contemporary marketing. The 1968 headset that followed was so heavy it had to be suspended from the ceiling, which is where its nickname comes from.

The early 1990s produced the field’s first consumer wave, and its first collapse. Virtuality arcade machines appeared in shopping centres, Sega announced and then cancelled a headset, and Nintendo shipped the Virtual Boy in 1995 to poor sales and widespread complaints of headaches. The technology could not yet deliver what the concept promised: displays were low resolution, tracking was slow, and latency was high enough to make many users unwell within minutes. The lesson is not that the ideas were wrong but that immersive systems fail ungracefully. A screen application that runs poorly is annoying; an immersive one that runs poorly makes people ill and they do not come back.

5.2 The First Consumer Wave (2012–2016)

  • 2012: Oculus Rift Kickstarter campaign raises $2.4 million
  • 2014: Facebook acquires Oculus for $2 billion
  • 2016: Consumer launches of Oculus Rift, HTC Vive, and PlayStation VR
  • 2016: Pokémon GO demonstrates mass-market AR

What changed between 1995 and 2012 was largely borrowed rather than invented. The smartphone industry had driven down the cost of high-density displays, accelerometers, gyroscopes and mobile processors to the point where a headset could be assembled from commodity parts. This is a recurring pattern in the field and a useful lens for evaluating claims about the future: ask which adjacent industry is paying for the component improvements.

Pokémon GO deserves particular attention because it is the most commercially successful AR product to date and it uses almost none of the technology this module covers. There was no registration to speak of, no depth sensing, no scene understanding. Its achievement was locational and social rather than perceptual, which is a useful corrective to the assumption that technical sophistication drives adoption.

5.3 Standalone and Spatial Computing (2019–present)

  • 2019: Meta Quest 1 launches — standalone VR without a PC
  • 2021: Meta rebrands from Facebook, announces metaverse strategy
  • 2022: Meta Quest Pro introduces mixed reality passthrough
  • 2023: Apple announces Vision Pro at WWDC
  • 2024: Spatial computing enters enterprise and consumer markets at scale

The significant transition here is the removal of the cable. Standalone headsets traded graphical fidelity for the elimination of a PC, a setup process and a tether, and that trade turned out to be strongly favourable for adoption. It also created the constraint you will spend Week 10 working within: a standalone headset is a mobile device with a mobile GPU, rendering two views at 72Hz or better, and your frame budget is correspondingly tight.

The current period is best understood as unresolved rather than settled. Headsets are capable but bulky; glasses are comfortable but limited; the compelling consumer application has not obviously arrived; and enterprise deployment is growing steadily but with less visibility. You are studying this field during a genuinely open moment, which is more interesting than studying a mature one.

6 Ten Years of Hype: Reading Expectations Against Reality

The timeline above tells you what happened. It does not tell you what people expected to happen at each point, and the gap between the two is where most of the field’s bad decisions — and a fair amount of good research funding — have come from. To read that gap, we can use Gartner’s Hype Cycle, a model of how expectations for an emerging technology tend to change over time, independently of how the underlying technology is actually progressing.

6.1 The Hype Cycle Model

Gartner’s model describes five phases that a technology typically passes through in the public and industry imagination:

  1. Innovation Trigger — a breakthrough, product launch or demonstration generates significant press interest
  2. Peak of Inflated Expectations — early publicity produces a number of success stories, often accompanied by scores of failures; expectations run well ahead of demonstrated capability
  3. Trough of Disillusionment — interest wanes as experiments and implementations fail to deliver; producers of the technology shake out or fail
  4. Slope of Enlightenment — more instances of how the technology can benefit the enterprise or consumer start to crystallise and become more widely understood
  5. Plateau of Productivity — mainstream adoption starts to take off; the technology’s broad market applicability and relevance are paying off

Before applying this to XR, it is worth being honest about what the model is and is not. It is a communication tool, not a measuring instrument — Gartner does not disclose a rigorous methodology for placing a technology at a specific point, and the chart has been criticised for being unfalsifiable, since almost any subsequent event can be narrated as fitting one phase or another. Treat what follows as a structured way of organising a history you already partly know, not as evidence of a natural law that immersive technologies must obey.

6.2 Virtual Reality: 2015 → 2018

VR entered its innovation trigger around the Oculus Kickstarter and the Facebook acquisition, climbed rapidly to a peak of inflated expectations around the 2016 consumer launches (Rift, Vive, PSVR), and then slid into a trough of disillusionment as consumer sales underwhelmed, content libraries remained thin, and the promised killer application did not arrive. The technology did not get worse during this period — if anything, standalone headsets were already in development — but the expectation collapsed faster than the hardware improved.

6.3 Augmented Reality: 2016 → 2019

AR followed a similar but offset arc, triggered by Pokémon GO’s runaway 2016 success and ARKit/ARCore’s 2017 launches, which briefly promised AR as the next mobile computing platform. Expectations peaked around 2017–2018 and then fell as it became clear that phone-based AR, without registration or persistence beyond a single session, was a novelty layer rather than a platform shift for most consumer use cases.

6.4 The Metaverse Spike: 2022 → 2023

The metaverse spike is the sharpest and most recent example in the chart, and the most useful one for you to study critically. Meta’s 2021 rebrand and subsequent investment triggered an intense peak of inflated expectations through 2022, with enormous capital committed to virtual real estate, corporate presence and social VR platforms. By 2023, user numbers, engagement figures and press sentiment had all fallen sharply, and much of that investment was quietly written down or redirected. Note what this spike measures: it says very little about whether headset hardware improved in that period (it did — Quest 3 launched in the middle of the trough), and a great deal about how a marketing narrative outran what the technology could support.

6.5 Spatial Computing: 2024 → 2025

The current reframing around “spatial computing”, prompted substantially by Apple’s Vision Pro announcement and launch, shows the early shape of a new innovation trigger and peak. It is deliberately positioned as distinct from the exhausted “VR” and “metaverse” branding, which is itself a useful data point: when a term becomes commercially damaged, the industry does not usually fix the underlying problem, it renames the category. Whether spatial computing repeats the same arc, plateaus faster because the underlying hardware and software ecosystem is more mature, or fails to trigger a peak at all, is an open question you are positioned to watch unfold during this module rather than read about after the fact.

6.6 Ten Years, One Chart

Plotted together, VR, AR, the metaverse and spatial computing do not form one clean curve — they form four overlapping cycles at different phases, which is closer to how the field actually behaves than any single narrative of “XR” as one technology on one trajectory. Some of these labels describe genuinely different capabilities; some describe the same underlying hardware rebranded to escape a damaged reputation. Distinguishing the two is a skill, not a fact you can look up.

6.7 What Leaving the Cycle Means

The most useful lesson from ten years of this chart is not where any single label currently sits, but what happens to a technology that survives its trough. A technology that reaches the plateau of productivity typically stops being discussed as a hype phenomenon at all — it simply becomes infrastructure. Nobody today writes hype-cycle commentary about the smartphone touchscreen or GPS. If spatial computing succeeds, the marker of that success will not be a triumphant peak; it will be the field’s vocabulary quietly disappearing from the technology press because the capability has become unremarkable. Judge claims about “where XR is heading” against that standard: a technology that is still being sold on its promise, rather than used without comment, has not yet reached the plateau, whatever the marketing implies.

6.8 Discussion: Hype and Evidence

Carry these questions with you through the rest of the module, and revisit them explicitly when you scope Assessment 2 and 3:

  • If expectations collapsed for the metaverse while the hardware (Quest 3, passthrough mixed reality) kept improving, what was the hype cycle chart actually measuring — the technology, or the narrative around it?
  • Is “spatial computing” a genuinely distinct technical paradigm from VR/AR/MR, or a rebranding exercise timed to distance the industry from the metaverse trough? What evidence would distinguish the two?
  • When you evaluate a piece of hardware or a research claim later in this module, how will you separate its demonstrated capability from the phase of the hype cycle its category currently occupies?

7 Degrees of Freedom

A fundamental concept in XR hardware is degrees of freedom (DOF), which describes how many independent axes of movement a tracking system can detect.

7.1 3DOF

Three degrees of freedom tracks rotation only:

  • pitch (nodding)
  • yaw (shaking head)
  • roll (tilting head)

3DOF systems cannot detect the user moving through space. They are suitable for 360-degree video viewing but not for interactive VR.

The perceptual consequence is worth experiencing rather than reading about. In a 3DOF system, leaning to look around an object does not work — the world rotates with you but does not shift, so the object stays stubbornly in front of you. Because your vestibular system correctly reports that you moved while your eyes report that the world did not respond, 3DOF systems tend to feel subtly wrong even to users who cannot articulate why, and they provoke discomfort more readily than 6DOF systems during any movement more energetic than sitting still.

7.2 6DOF

Six degrees of freedom tracks rotation and position:

  • pitch, yaw, roll (rotation)
  • x, y, z (position in space)

6DOF allows the user to physically move through the virtual environment. All primary devices in this module support 6DOF tracking.

6DOF is what makes parallax work, and parallax is a large part of why virtual objects read as solid and located rather than as images. It is also what makes physical crouching, leaning and stepping available as interaction techniques — a design vocabulary with no screen-based equivalent.

Note that head tracking and controller tracking are separate questions. A headset may be 6DOF while its controllers are 3DOF, which was common in earlier standalone devices and immediately rules out direct manipulation of objects in space.

8 Tracking Technologies

8.1 Inside-Out Tracking

Cameras mounted on the headset observe the environment and use computer vision to determine the headset’s position and orientation. This approach requires no external hardware and is used by the Meta Quest 2, Quest 3, and HoloLens 2.

Advantages:

  • no external setup required
  • portable and flexible

Limitations:

  • can struggle in low-light or featureless environments
  • tracking volume limited by camera field of view

The mechanism underneath is worth naming now because Week 7 returns to it in detail. Inside-out tracking is a form of SLAM — simultaneous localisation and mapping. The device identifies visual features in the environment, builds a sparse map of them, and continuously estimates its own pose relative to that map. Two practical consequences follow.

The first is that tracking quality depends on the room. A space with plenty of visual texture — furniture, posters, patterned surfaces — tracks well. A blank white studio, a dark room, or a space dominated by reflective or repetitive surfaces tracks poorly, because the system cannot find or distinguish features. This is a common cause of “the headset is broken” reports that are actually room problems.

The second is drift. Small errors in pose estimation accumulate, so a virtual object left in place may be a few centimetres away from where it was after a period of use. Systems correct for this by recognising previously mapped areas, a process called relocalisation, which is also how content can be restored to the right place in a new session.

8.2 Outside-In Tracking

External sensors (base stations or cameras) track the headset and controllers. The HTC Vive Pro uses this approach with SteamVR base stations.

Advantages:

  • highly accurate and reliable
  • large tracking volume possible

Limitations:

  • requires setup and calibration
  • not portable

Outside-in tracking has largely lost the consumer market to inside-out, but it persists where precision matters more than convenience: motion capture, research settings, location-based entertainment and professional training installations. It also has a genuine technical advantage in that it does not depend on the visual texture of the room, and it does not suffer the same drift behaviour, since position is measured against fixed external references rather than an accumulated internal map.

9 The Hardware in This Module

You will have access to a range of devices throughout this module. Each offers different capabilities and design constraints. Week 2 examines this landscape comparatively; the purpose here is simply to know what is in the room.

9.1 Meta Quest 2

Standalone, 6DOF, inside-out tracking, touch controllers with haptics, and hand tracking. Still widely used in industry and education, and accepted for any session in this module that does not need colour passthrough.

9.2 Meta Quest 3

The primary target platform for this module. Adds full-colour passthrough for mixed reality, improved processing, and a slimmer form factor. Required rather than preferred from Week 7, where the mixed reality and scene understanding work depends on passthrough and room capture.

9.3 HTC Vive Pro

A PC-tethered headset with outside-in tracking via SteamVR base stations. Higher visual fidelity than the Quest 2. Useful for understanding the trade-offs between standalone and tethered systems.

9.4 Pico Neo Eye

Includes integrated eye tracking hardware. Enables gaze-based interaction and foveated rendering — relevant to the gaze input discussion in Week 4 and to the rendering techniques covered in Week 10.

9.5 HoloLens 2

Microsoft’s mixed reality headset. Optical see-through display, hand tracking, gaze tracking, voice input. The clearest available demonstration of the optical see-through constraints described above, and a useful comparison point for the spatial mapping work in Week 7.

9.6 Snap Spectacles 2022

Lightweight AR glasses with a small display. Useful for understanding the form factor trade-off between glasses and headsets, and available alongside Lens Studio as a platform option for Assessment 2.

9.7 Xreal One Pro AR Display Glasses

Lightweight tethered AR display glasses presenting a virtual screen without full world tracking. These present a virtual screen without tracking the world, which makes them a useful illustration of where “AR glasses” marketing outruns capability.

10 Why XR Design is Different

Designing for XR requires a fundamentally different mindset from designing for screens.

10.1 The body is the interface

In XR, the user’s physical body — their hands, head, gaze, voice, and movement — becomes the primary input device. Design must account for physical ergonomics, fatigue, and the limits of human movement.

The practical implication is that interaction has a physical cost that accumulates. An interface element placed slightly too high is a minor annoyance on the first use and a source of genuine shoulder fatigue on the fiftieth. Screen interfaces do not degrade this way, and the habit of ignoring physical cost is one of the harder ones to unlearn. Week 4 gives this a name — gorilla arm — and a measurement method.

10.2 Space is the canvas

Rather than arranging elements on a flat surface, XR designers work in three-dimensional space. Objects have position, scale, and orientation. The user can move around them, look at them from different angles, and interact with them physically.

You also lose control of the camera. In film and in most games, the designer decides what the viewer sees and when; in XR, the user decides, and taking that control back is one of the most reliable ways to make someone unwell. Everything you might have done with a cut or a camera move must instead be done with composition, light, sound and motion. Week 8 is largely about the techniques that replace the camera.

10.3 Presence changes behaviour

Research has shown that people behave differently in immersive environments than they do in front of screens. They respond emotionally to virtual stimuli, feel genuine discomfort in threatening virtual situations, and can develop real skills through virtual practice. This makes XR a powerful tool — and a significant ethical responsibility.

The same mechanism that makes immersive training effective makes immersive persuasion effective, and the user has fewer defences than they do against a screen: they cannot easily look away, the content surrounds them, and the system knows where they are looking. Week 12 addresses this directly, but it is worth holding in mind from the first prototype rather than treating ethics as a topic that arrives near the end of term.

10.4 Comfort is a design constraint

Unlike screen-based interfaces, XR can cause physical discomfort — motion sickness, eye strain, neck fatigue, and disorientation. Good XR design treats comfort as a first-class design requirement, not an afterthought.

Two specific mechanisms are worth naming now. Vergence-accommodation conflict arises because your eyes converge on a virtual object at its apparent distance while remaining focused on the physical display a few centimetres away; the mismatch contributes to eye strain, particularly for near content. Frame timing matters because a dropped frame delays the world’s response to head movement, producing exactly the sensory conflict that causes nausea. Both are covered properly later — Week 3 for the perceptual account, Week 10 for the engineering — but note that comfort is where the perceptual and technical halves of this module meet, and that a comfort failure is not a polish issue you can defer.

11 The Module in Context

This is a third-year module. You are expected to bring critical thinking, not just technical skill. Throughout the module, you will be asked to:

  • analyse existing XR experiences, not just build new ones
  • justify your design decisions with reference to theory and evidence
  • evaluate your work honestly, including its limitations
  • consider the ethical implications of immersive design

Concretely, the semester runs as two parallel tracks. The weekly lecture builds the conceptual and analytical vocabulary — what presence is, how perception works, which frameworks let you say precisely why an interaction fails. The practical and laboratory sessions extend your Unity competence on the hardware. The two are deliberately coupled: the concept introduced on the Monday is generally the thing you build or measure later in the week.

The assessment structure follows the same logic. Assessment 1 runs continuously and rewards evidenced practical work. Assessment 2, at the midpoint, asks you to look outward at an existing experience and deconstruct it, then prove the analysis by rebuilding one of its interactions. Assessment 3 is your own project, where you set the concept and defend the decisions. A recurring theme in the marking criteria is that a design choice you can justify is worth more than a technically impressive one you cannot.
