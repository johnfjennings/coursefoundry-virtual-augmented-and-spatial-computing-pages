The XR Hardware & Platform Ecosystem

1 Last Week Recap

In Week 1 we established the conceptual landscape for this module: the XR spectrum (VR, AR, MR, and spatial computing), the reality-virtuality continuum (Milgram et al. 1994), 3DOF vs 6DOF tracking, inside-out vs outside-in tracking, and a brief history running from Sutherland’s “Ultimate Display” (Sutherland 1965) to the Apple Vision Pro.

This week we move from concepts to concrete hardware. Our goal is not to memorise specifications, but to build a critical vocabulary you can use to evaluate any XR device — including ones that do not exist yet.

This lecture directly supports LO2: explore, critique, and deconstruct modern VR, AR, and spatial computing experiences.

3 Standalone VR: Meta Quest 3 / 3S

The Meta Quest 3 and Quest 3S represent the current mainstream of standalone VR — headsets with all compute, battery, and sensors onboard, requiring no PC or console tether.

3.1 Optics

  • The Quest 3 uses pancake lenses, which fold light path through polarisation to produce a thinner, sharper optical stack with less distortion at the edges.
  • The Quest 3S uses more traditional Fresnel lenses, which are cheaper to manufacture but introduce more “god rays” (visible light scatter) and a bulkier form factor.

This is a useful first lesson in hardware critique: two devices from the same manufacturer, released in the same product generation, can make very different optical trade-offs to hit different price points.

It is worth understanding why these two lens types behave differently, because the same physics recurs across every device in this module.

A Fresnel lens approximates a thick convex lens by collapsing it into concentric ridges. This makes it light and cheap, but each ridge boundary is a discontinuity that scatters light. When a bright object sits on a dark background — white text on black, a moon in a night sky — that scatter appears as radial streaks, the “god rays” users complain about. Fresnel lenses also have a small sweet spot: the image is sharp near the optical centre and degrades toward the edges, which is why users are told to adjust the fit until the image “clicks” into focus.

A pancake lens instead folds the light path back on itself two or three times inside a thin stack, using polarisation to control which pass exits toward the eye. The optical path length is preserved while the physical depth is not, which is what allows a thinner headset with a much larger sweet spot and far less edge distortion. The cost is light: each fold discards a large proportion of the light — typically most of it — so pancake optics require a substantially brighter and more power-hungry display to reach the same perceived brightness. Pancake optics are therefore a choice that spends battery and cost to buy comfort and clarity.

The lesson generalises. Optical designs in XR are not ranked from worse to better; they are trades between clarity, weight, brightness, power and price, and a manufacturer’s choice tells you which of those they prioritised for that product.

3.2 Passthrough and sensing

Both devices offer full-colour passthrough — external cameras feed a live video composite of the real world to the internal displays — plus depth sensing that allows virtual objects to be occluded correctly by real furniture and walls. This is what allows the Quest 3 family to market itself as “mixed reality” capable, not purely VR.

Passthrough is harder than it sounds, and understanding why explains most of its visible shortcomings.

The cameras are not where your eyes are. They sit on the front of the headset, several centimetres forward and apart from your actual eye positions, so the raw camera image has the wrong viewpoint. The system must reproject that image to your eyes’ perspective, which requires depth information — and where depth is wrong or missing, you get the warping around hands and door frames that users notice immediately.

Everything in the pipeline costs time. Capture, reprojection, composition and display each add latency, and the real world is now subject to that budget. A small delay between moving your head and the room responding is tolerable; a large one is nauseating, for exactly the reasons covered in Week 3.

Camera sensors also have far less dynamic range than the eye. A room with a bright window will show either a blown-out window or a dark interior, rarely both, and low-light performance is noticeably grainy. When you evaluate passthrough quality in the lab, look specifically at high-contrast scenes and at your own hands — those are where the pipeline shows its limits.

3.3 Input

  • Inside-out tracked controllers (retaining physical buttons, triggers, and haptics)
  • Camera-based hand tracking (no controllers required, lower fidelity, no haptic feedback)

Controllers are tracked by the same headset cameras that track the world, which has one important consequence: a controller the cameras cannot see is not tracked visually. Behind your back, close to your chest, or held against your body, the system falls back on the controller’s inertial sensors, which drift within about a second. This is why designers avoid interactions that require reaching behind the body, and why the Week 5 wrist menu sits where the cameras can see it.

NoteWhy this matters

Standalone hardware removed the PC-tether barrier that constrained the first consumer VR wave (2016). This is arguably the single biggest driver of mass XR adoption to date — cost, setup complexity, and physical tethering were the primary barriers identified in early VR adoption research.

4 Mixed Reality Headsets

4.1 Apple Vision Pro

Apple explicitly avoids calling the Vision Pro a VR or AR headset — it is marketed as a “spatial computing” device. This is a deliberate positioning choice, not just a technical one.

Key characteristics:

  • visionOS introduces a new interaction paradigm built around windows, volumes, and spaces — 2D app windows, 3D volumetric content, and fully immersive environments can coexist
  • Eye tracking + pinch gesture input replaces controllers as the primary interaction model — you look at what you want and pinch to select
  • Uses passthrough (video see-through), not optical see-through, despite the “spatial computing” framing

The eye-tracking-plus-pinch model deserves attention because it is the clearest current answer to a problem we return to in Week 4: gaze is fast and effortless for pointing, but terrible for selecting, because we look at things constantly without meaning to act on them. Separating the two channels — eyes to indicate, hand to commit — sidesteps that. It also has a privacy dimension that Apple treats seriously: where a user looked is not reported to the application, only what they selected, because gaze data reveals attention, interest and arousal in ways users have no intuition about. We return to that in Week 12.

4.2 HoloLens 2

Microsoft’s HoloLens 2 takes a different technical approach entirely:

  • Optical see-through waveguide display — the user looks directly through a transparent lens with light guided into the eye, meaning the real world is never digitised or delayed
  • Narrower field of view than consumer VR headsets, a direct consequence of waveguide optics
  • Strong hand tracking and eye/gaze tracking, voice input
  • Positioned squarely for enterprise and industrial use cases (remote assistance, training, field service) rather than consumer entertainment

A waveguide works by injecting an image into the edge of a transparent pane, bouncing it along by total internal reflection, and extracting it toward the eye with a diffraction grating. The real world passes straight through the glass, which is why there is no latency and no camera pipeline. The costs are structural rather than incidental: gratings are wavelength-dependent, so colour uniformity is difficult; only a fraction of the injected light reaches the eye, so the display must be bright and the image still washes out in daylight; and the field of view is limited by the physics of the extraction optics rather than by cost. This is why optical see-through devices have narrow fields of view and are unlikely to catch up with headsets in that respect soon.

The consequence for you as a designer is the one raised in Week 1: additive light cannot produce black. On HoloLens, dark UI is invisible, drop shadows do nothing, and a “dimmed” background does not dim. Content has to be designed as luminous, high-contrast, and sparse.

4.3 Passthrough vs Optical See-Through

This is one of the most important technical distinctions in the whole module, because it explains many downstream differences in latency, fidelity, and use case.

Passthrough (Video See-Through) Optical See-Through
How it works Cameras capture the real world; a composite image is rendered and displayed on internal screens A transparent lens/waveguide overlays digital content directly onto the user’s natural view
Examples Quest 3, Vision Pro HoloLens 2
Real-world fidelity Limited by camera resolution, dynamic range, and field of view Perfect — the user sees real, direct light
Latency risk Camera-to-display lag can cause a subtle disconnect between real and virtual None for the real-world view; only virtual content can lag
Occlusion of virtual content Straightforward (composited digitally) Harder — virtual light is added on top of real light, so true occlusion of virtual-behind-real is difficult

Two further consequences follow from the table and are worth stating explicitly.

Safety and failure behaviour differ. If an optical see-through device loses power or crashes, the user is looking through clear glass and can walk away. If a passthrough device does the same, the user is blind until they lift the headset. This matters for any application in a workplace, near machinery, or with vulnerable users.

Social visibility differs. Optical devices generally let others see the wearer’s eyes. Passthrough devices do not, which is why Vision Pro renders a synthetic image of the wearer’s eyes on an external display — an expensive engineering solution to a purely social problem, and a good illustration that hardware decisions in this field are frequently about acceptability rather than capability.

5 AR Glasses: Xreal One Pro and Peers

A third hardware category sits apart from headsets altogether: lightweight, glasses-form-factor AR devices such as the Xreal One Pro.

Characteristics:

  • Designed to look and feel like ordinary glasses — lightweight, low profile, socially acceptable to wear in public
  • Typically tethered to a phone, laptop, or dedicated puck for compute and battery
  • Use birdbath or waveguide optics to project a floating virtual screen in the user’s field of view — commonly used as a private, portable second monitor
  • Some newer models add spatial/positional tracking so content can be anchored to the room rather than simply following the user’s head
  • Optimised for portability and casual, short-duration wear — not for deep immersion or spatial interaction

Birdbath optics are worth distinguishing from waveguides, since both appear in this category. A birdbath design reflects the display off a beamsplitter into a curved mirror and back to the eye. It gives better colour and contrast than a waveguide at much lower cost, which is why media-viewing glasses use it — but the optical stack is bulkier and the transparency of the real world is reduced, so these devices look more like tinted sunglasses than clear lenses.

The critical question to ask of any device in this category is whether it tracks the world at all. Many “AR glasses” are 3DOF display devices: they show a screen that follows your head, with no knowledge of the room. That is a legitimate product — a private monitor is useful — but it is not augmented reality in the sense Azuma defined it, which requires registration in three dimensions (Azuma 1997). When you evaluate these in the lab, establish first whether content stays put when you walk around it.

5.1 AR Glasses vs Headsets

Factor AR Glasses (e.g. Xreal One Pro) MR/VR Headsets (e.g. Quest 3, Vision Pro)
Compute Tethered to a host device Onboard (standalone)
Field of view Narrow, screen-like Wide, immersive
Interaction Minimal — largely passive viewing Rich — hands, gaze, controllers
Primary use case Screen replacement, private media viewing Games, training, spatial computing, social VR

6 The Numbers That Actually Matter

Specification sheets are written to sell devices, and the most prominent numbers are frequently the least informative. Three in particular are worth being able to interpret.

6.1 Resolution, and why it misleads

A headset advertised at “4K per eye” sounds directly comparable to a 4K television, and is not. On a television, those pixels are spread across a screen occupying perhaps 30 degrees of your vision. In a headset, they are spread across 100 degrees or more. The same pixel count is stretched over three times the angular area, so the perceived sharpness is far lower than the number suggests.

The meaningful metric is pixels per degree (PPD) — how many pixels fall within one degree of your visual field. Current consumer headsets sit around 20–35 PPD. Human foveal vision resolves roughly 60 PPD, which is the informal target for “retinal” quality and which no consumer headset has yet reached. When comparing two devices, PPD tells you which will look sharper; raw resolution alone does not, because it says nothing about how widely those pixels are spread.

Low PPD produces the screen-door effect, where the unlit gaps between pixels become visible as a fine grid. It has largely receded on current hardware but is still noticeable on bright, uniform surfaces.

6.2 Field of view, and why quoted figures disagree

FOV can be quoted horizontally, vertically, or diagonally, and manufacturers naturally quote whichever is largest — usually the diagonal. Two devices claiming “110 degrees” may differ substantially in the horizontal figure that actually determines how much of a scene you see without turning your head.

There is also binocular overlap — the portion of the field both eyes see, which is where stereoscopic depth is available. A wide total FOV with poor overlap gives peripheral awareness but a narrower region of genuine depth perception.

For AR specifically, FOV is the dominant complaint about optical see-through devices, and it is a physics constraint rather than a cost one. Content that extends beyond the display simply stops at a hard edge, which is far more noticeable than a peripheral limit in VR because the real world continues around it.

6.3 Refresh rate

Quoted in hertz — commonly 72, 90 or 120. This is not a smoothness preference as it would be on a monitor; it sets the ceiling on motion-to-photon latency, the delay between moving your head and the display responding. Below roughly 20 milliseconds, most users are comfortable; above it, discomfort rises quickly.

The number on the box is the display’s capability. Whether your application actually achieves it is a separate question, and the subject of Week 10.

6.4 Panel technology

  • LCD — cheaper, good brightness and pixel fill, but weaker blacks and contrast. Used in the Quest series.
  • OLED / micro-OLED — true blacks, high contrast, very fast response; more expensive, and micro-OLED enables the small, dense panels that pancake optics and high PPD require. Used in Vision Pro.

Panel choice interacts with optics: pancake lenses discard most of the light, so they pair naturally with the brightness available from micro-OLED.

7 Compute, Thermals, and Battery

A standalone headset is a mobile device. It runs a phone-class system-on-chip, rendering two views of your scene, every frame, while simultaneously running computer vision for tracking, hand detection and passthrough. That workload is substantial before your application does anything at all.

Three consequences follow, and all three will affect your Assessment 3 project.

Your frame budget is small. At 72Hz you have under 14 milliseconds per frame for everything, and the system has already spent part of it. This is why Week 10 treats performance as a comfort requirement rather than polish, and why techniques such as foveated rendering exist.

Thermal throttling is real. Sustained load heats the device, and the SoC reduces its clock speed to stay within thermal limits. A build that runs acceptably for five minutes may degrade noticeably after twenty. Test sessions long enough to see this, because your users will.

Battery constrains session design. Two to three hours is typical, less under heavy load. An experience intended for a classroom or exhibition has to account for charging, and for the fact that a warm headset is an uncomfortable one.

Tethered devices — the Vive Pro, or glasses tethered to a puck — trade mobility for a larger power and thermal envelope, which is precisely why they retain an advantage in visual fidelity. This is the same trade appearing again in a different form.

8 Platform SDKs and App Stores

Hardware is only half the story. The software platform a device runs on determines what developers can build, how it is distributed, and how it is reviewed.

8.1 Meta Horizon OS

  • Built around the Meta XR SDK, with support for Unity, Unreal, and native development
  • Distribution via the Meta Horizon Store (curated, reviewed) and App Lab (lighter-touch, more experimental distribution)
  • Publishing requires meeting comfort ratings and performance benchmarks before an app can be listed

That last point is unusual and worth dwelling on. Meta will reject an application for failing to hold its frame rate. There are very few software platforms where performance is a review criterion rather than a quality preference — and the reason is the one established in Week 1, that a slow immersive application does not merely feel sluggish, it makes people unwell.

8.2 visionOS

  • Built around RealityKit and SwiftUI, developed almost exclusively through Xcode
  • Distribution via the Apple App Store, subject to Apple’s standard app review process
  • Apple publishes spatial-specific Human Interface Guidelines (HIG) that developers are expected to follow — covering topics like comfortable interaction distances, eye-tracking privacy, and window behaviour

8.3 OpenXR and the portability question

Both platforms above are vendor ecosystems, and building directly against either creates lock-in. OpenXR is the cross-vendor standard intended to address this: an application written against OpenXR can, in principle, run on any conformant runtime.

In practice, portability is partial. OpenXR covers the common core — sessions, spaces, input actions, rendering — but every vendor exposes distinguishing features through extensions, and those features are frequently the ones you actually want. Scene understanding, passthrough configuration and hand-tracking detail are typically vendor extensions. The realistic position is that OpenXR makes porting substantially cheaper, not free, and that is the assumption you should carry into a platform recommendation.

This is why your Unity projects in this module target OpenXR with the Meta feature group enabled: the standard underneath, the vendor’s capabilities on top.

9 A Framework for Critical Comparison

Rather than memorising specification sheets, use these four criteria to evaluate any XR device you encounter — in this module, in industry, or in a device that has not been released yet:

  1. Comfort — weight, weight distribution, fit, and how long a typical user can comfortably use it in one session
  2. Field of view (FOV) — measured in degrees; affects edge clipping and how much of a task or environment is visible without turning the head
  3. Tracking fidelity — the accuracy, latency, and robustness of head, hand, and/or eye tracking, especially in non-ideal conditions
  4. Ecosystem maturity — the strength of the SDK, the size and quality of the app store or distribution channel, and the size of the developer and content community

These four criteria will recur throughout the module whenever we introduce a new device or platform.

9.1 Using the framework honestly

Two notes on applying it.

First, weight distribution matters more than weight. A heavier headset with a rear counterweight is frequently more comfortable over an hour than a lighter one that hangs entirely off the face. Total mass is the number on the spec sheet; the distribution is what the user feels. This is exactly the kind of judgement that only comes from wearing the device, which is what this week’s lab is for.

Second, tracking fidelity should be assessed under adverse conditions, not ideal ones. Every device tracks well in a well-lit, textured room while standing still. The differences appear in dim light, near blank walls, with fast head movement, or with a controller held out of camera view.

9.2 What the framework leaves out

No four-item framework is complete, and being able to say what yours omits is part of using it critically. Three candidates you may wish to add, depending on the brief:

  • Price and total cost of ownership — including controllers, facial interfaces, charging infrastructure and the replacement cost when a student drops one
  • Content and tooling availability — a technically superior device with no engine support and no content is not the better choice for a twelve-week project
  • Social acceptability and privacy — whether the device can reasonably be worn in front of other people, and what it records about the wearer and the room while doing so

The fourth criterion, ecosystem maturity, partly captures the second of these, but the framework as stated has nothing to say about cost or privacy — and in a real client recommendation, cost usually decides.

10 Reading a Spec Sheet Critically

A short checklist you can apply to any device announcement:

  1. Is the resolution given with a field of view? Without both, you cannot compute PPD, and the resolution figure alone tells you little.
  2. Is the FOV horizontal or diagonal? If unstated, assume diagonal, which is the more flattering figure.
  3. Is the refresh rate the display’s maximum or a sustained figure? These are different claims.
  4. Is tracking 6DOF for the headset only, or for the controllers too?
  5. Does it understand the room, or only show it? Passthrough is not scene understanding.
  6. What is the battery figure measured doing? Idle, video playback and interactive rendering give very different numbers.
  7. What is missing entirely? Weight distribution, thermal behaviour under sustained load, and daylight legibility are rarely in the marketing material and frequently decide whether a device is usable.

11 Key Questions to Carry Forward

  • Is “spatial computing” a genuinely distinct technical category, or primarily a marketing category used to differentiate Apple’s device?
  • Does the “best” device for a given use case have to be the most technically impressive one?
  • How much should ecosystem maturity weigh against raw hardware specifications when recommending a platform to a client or employer?
  • If optical see-through cannot render black and cannot match a headset’s field of view, what class of applications is it nonetheless the right choice for?

Keep these questions in mind for this week’s lab and practical — you will be asked to take a position and justify it.

15 Summary

  • Standalone VR (Quest 3/3S), MR headsets (Vision Pro, HoloLens 2), and AR glasses (Xreal One Pro) represent three genuinely distinct hardware philosophies, not just different price points of the same idea
  • Optical designs — Fresnel, pancake, waveguide, birdbath — are trades between clarity, weight, brightness, power and cost, not a ranking from worse to better
  • Passthrough vs optical see-through is a key technical and experiential distinction with knock-on effects for latency, fidelity, occlusion, safety and social visibility
  • Pixels per degree, not raw resolution, determines perceived sharpness; quoted FOV figures are usually diagonal
  • A standalone headset is a mobile device with a mobile thermal and power budget, and that budget is the constraint behind most performance advice in this module
  • Platform choice — Horizon OS vs visionOS — shapes tooling, review processes, and distribution; OpenXR reduces lock-in but does not eliminate it
  • Comfort, FOV, tracking fidelity, and ecosystem maturity together form a repeatable, transferable framework for critiquing any XR device — provided you can also say what it leaves out
