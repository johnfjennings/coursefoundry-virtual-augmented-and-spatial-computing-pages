Practical

Locomotion Comfort Study

Author

Mr. John Jennings

Overview

Duration: 2 hours Software: Unity 6.3 LTS, XR Interaction Toolkit 3.x (Starter Assets sample), OpenXR Hardware: Meta Quest 2 / 3 with controllers, plus the paper-prototyping materials listed in Part 3

Locomotion is the single largest cause of discomfort in XR, and developers are systematically the worst judges of it – prolonged exposure builds tolerance most users do not have. This practical replaces your own tolerance with peer data, and prototypes spatial UI on paper before any of it is built.

Objectives

By the end of this practical, you will be able to:

  • Implement and compare two locomotion schemes against peer comfort ratings
  • Tune a comfort profile from user feedback rather than personal tolerance
  • Sketch and validate spatial UI at true scale before implementation

Prerequisites

  • The Unity 6.3 project you configured for Quest in the Week 4 practical: OpenXR with the Meta Quest feature group, XR Interaction Toolkit 3.x and its Starter Assets sample imported. If you are starting fresh, follow Week 4’s “Before the Session: Project Setup” first.
  • A completed Week 5 consent briefing. You are about to deliberately make peers uncomfortable, so the duty-of-care rules apply.
  • The paper-prototyping materials listed in Part 3
WarningLocomotion changed in XRI 3

If a tutorial mentions a Locomotion System, Action-based Continuous Move Provider or Device-based Snap Turn Provider, it is written for XRI 2.x. In 3.x a Locomotion Mediator and an XR Body Transformer sit on the rig, and there is a single provider of each kind: Teleportation Provider, Continuous Move Provider (the Starter Assets use its subclass, Dynamic Move Provider), Snap Turn Provider and Continuous Turn Provider.

Instructions

Part 1: Implement Two Schemes (40 minutes)

1.1 Build the Test Environment (10 minutes)

  1. Create the Scene
    • In your Week 4 project, create a new scene, Week5_Locomotion, and delete its default Main Camera.
    • From Samples/XR Interaction Toolkit/<version>/Starter Assets/Prefabs, drag XR Origin (XR Rig) into the scene at the origin.
    • Look inside the rig at Locomotion. Its children are the providers you will use: Turn (Snap Turn Provider and Continuous Turn Provider), Move (Dynamic Move Provider), Teleportation (Teleportation Provider), plus Grab Move and Climb, which you can ignore.
  2. Lay Out a Route
    • Add a Plane scaled to (4, 1, 4) (a 40 m × 40 m floor). Add a few cubes as walls and obstacles so the route cannot be walked in a straight line.
    • Mark a route of about 30 m with five numbered markers (thin cylinders or floor decals): Start → 1 → 2 → 3 → Finish. Include at least one 90° turn and one 180° turn, because turning is where most discomfort appears.
    • Every participant walks this same route under both schemes, so do not change it after Part 2 begins.
TipShort of time?

Starter Assets/Prefabs/Teleportation Environment.prefab is a ready-made floor with teleport areas and anchors. Drop it in and add your obstacles and markers to it.

1.2 Scheme A: Teleportation (12 minutes)

  1. Make the Floor a Teleport Target
    • Select the floor plane and add a Teleportation Area component. The plane’s Mesh Collider is what the teleport ray hits.
    • Set its Interaction Layer Mask to Teleport. The Starter Assets’ teleport ray only targets that layer. If Teleport is missing from the list, open Edit > Project Settings > XR Plug-in Management > Project Validation and apply the XR Interaction Toolkit fix that adds it.
    • Leave obstacles without a Teleportation Area, so they become invalid targets. The ray turns red over them, which is the “valid/invalid zone” feedback from the lecture.
  2. Add Two Teleport Anchors
    • GameObject > XR > Teleportation Anchor. Place one at marker 2 and one at the Finish. Set each anchor’s Interaction Layer Mask to Teleport as well.
    • Rotate each anchor so its blue (forward) arrow faces where the participant should look on arrival. Set Match Orientation to Target Up And Forward, so landing on an anchor also sets the user’s facing.
  3. Select Teleport Mode
    • Select Left Controller and Right Controller under the rig. On each one’s Controller Input Action Manager, untick Smooth Motion Enabled (this enables teleport) and untick Smooth Turn Enabled (this gives snap turns).
  4. Test It
    • Build and Run. Push the thumbstick forward to show the teleport arc, aim, and release to teleport. Push it left or right to snap-turn.

1.3 Scheme B: Smooth Locomotion (10 minutes)

  1. Switch to Smooth Movement
    • On both controllers’ Controller Input Action Manager, tick Smooth Motion Enabled. The left thumbstick now moves continuously and the teleport ray is disabled.
    • Decide on turning: tick Smooth Turn Enabled for continuous turning, or leave it unticked for snap turns. This is one of the variables you will tune.
  2. Set the Starting Values
    • Locomotion › Move › Dynamic Move Provider: set Move Speed to 2 m/s. The Starter Assets preset uses 2.5, which is fast for first-time users. Leave Enable Strafe ticked.
    • Locomotion › Turn › Snap Turn Provider: Turn Amount 45°.
    • Locomotion › Turn › Continuous Turn Provider: Turn Speed 60°/s.
  3. Add the Tunnelling Vignette
    • Find TunnelingVignette.prefab in the XR Interaction Toolkit samples (Starter Assets/TunnelingVignette). Drag it onto XR Origin › Camera Offset › Main Camera, so it becomes a child of the camera.
    • On its Tunneling Vignette Controller, add to Locomotion Vignette Providers the Dynamic Move Provider, the Continuous Turn Provider and the Snap Turn Provider.
    • Starting values: Aperture Size 0.7 (1 = no vignette, lower = narrower view), Feathering Effect 0.2, Ease In Time 0.3, Ease Out Time 0.3.
    • Optional, for Scheme A: also add the Teleportation Provider, tick Override Default Parameters, set Aperture Size 0, and set the Teleportation Provider’s Delay Time to 0.15 s. The view now blinks shut for each jump, which is the “fade transition” from the lecture.

1.4 Expose the Comfort Settings (8 minutes)

You will change settings between participants, so put them all on one component instead of hunting through the hierarchy. Create an empty GameObject, ComfortSettings, add this script, and drag in the references.

ComfortSettings.cs
using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion.Comfort;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion.Movement;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion.Turning;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Samples.StarterAssets;

public class ComfortSettings : MonoBehaviour
{
    public enum Scheme { Teleport, Smooth }

    [Header("Scheme")]
    [SerializeField] Scheme scheme = Scheme.Teleport;
    [SerializeField] bool smoothTurn = false;

    [Header("Tuning")]
    [SerializeField, Range(0.5f, 4f)] float moveSpeed = 2f;          // metres per second
    [SerializeField, Range(15f, 90f)] float snapTurnAngle = 45f;     // degrees per snap
    [SerializeField, Range(30f, 180f)] float smoothTurnSpeed = 60f;  // degrees per second
    [SerializeField, Range(0f, 1f)] float vignetteAperture = 0.7f;   // 1 = no vignette

    [Header("References")]
    [SerializeField] ControllerInputActionManager[] controllers;     // Left and Right Controller
    [SerializeField] ContinuousMoveProvider moveProvider;            // Locomotion › Move
    [SerializeField] SnapTurnProvider snapTurn;                      // Locomotion › Turn
    [SerializeField] ContinuousTurnProvider continuousTurn;          // Locomotion › Turn
    [SerializeField] TunnelingVignetteController vignette;           // under Main Camera

    void Start() => Apply();

    // Lets you change values in the Inspector during Play mode (e.g. over Quest Link)
    void OnValidate()
    {
        if (Application.isPlaying)
            Apply();
    }

    public void Apply()
    {
        foreach (var controller in controllers)
        {
            controller.smoothMotionEnabled = scheme == Scheme.Smooth;
            controller.smoothTurnEnabled = smoothTurn;
        }
        moveProvider.moveSpeed = moveSpeed;
        snapTurn.turnAmount = snapTurnAngle;
        continuousTurn.turnSpeed = smoothTurnSpeed;
        vignette.defaultParameters.apertureSize = vignetteAperture;

        Debug.Log($"Comfort settings: {scheme}, smoothTurn={smoothTurn}, speed={moveSpeed} m/s, " +
                  $"snap={snapTurnAngle}°, turnSpeed={smoothTurnSpeed}°/s, aperture={vignetteAperture}");
    }
}
  • Fastest way to tune: on Windows, connect the headset with Meta Horizon Link and press Play. You can then change values in the Inspector between runs without rebuilding. Otherwise, change the values and Build And Run again, which takes a minute or two per change.
  • Write the settings for every run on the recording sheet. The Debug.Log line gives you an exact copy in the Console.

Part 2: Peer Comfort Study (45 minutes)

  1. Prepare
    • Each participant completes the route once per scheme. Alternate which scheme goes first: participant 1 does A then B, participant 2 does B then A, participant 3 does A then B.
    • Use the Fast Motion Sickness Scale (FMS) for the comfort rating: “On a scale of 0 (no sickness at all) to 20 (frank sickness), how do you feel right now?” Ask it before the first run and at every marker during each run, so you capture how discomfort builds. FMS is quick enough to answer without leaving the headset (Keshavarz & Hecht, 2011).
    • Stop rule: end a run at once if FMS reaches 10 or more, or if the participant asks to stop. Remove the headset, let them sit down, and do not start the second scheme until their FMS is back to its starting value.
  2. Run the Comparison
    • The participant stands for both schemes. Note any stepping, swaying or grabbing for support.
    • Record for each run: time to complete, FMS at each marker, peak FMS, and the exact comfort settings.
    • After both runs, ask which scheme they preferred and why. Write down any spontaneous comment word for word.
    • Allow at least 5 minutes of rest out of the headset between the two schemes.

  3. Tune and Re-Test
    • Change one setting at a time in response to the feedback, for example vignette aperture 0.7 → 0.5, smooth turn → snap turn, or speed 2 → 1.5 m/s. Changing several at once means you cannot tell which change helped.
    • Re-test with at least one participant who found the smooth scheme uncomfortable (after their FMS has returned to baseline), and record whether their peak FMS fell.

Part 3: Spatial UI Paper Prototype (35 minutes)

Materials Required

Item Quantity (per pair) Used for
A4 card, 250–300 gsm (white and one colour) 3 sheets The menu panel and buttons
Sticky notes, 38 × 51 mm or smaller 1 pad Movable buttons and labels
Metre rule or tape measure 1 Measuring arm’s length and eye distance
30 cm ruler 1 Measuring and ruling the panel
Scissors, or craft knife with a cutting mat 1 set Cutting card to size
Pencil and black fine-liner (0.5 mm) 1 each Sketching, then inking final labels
Masking tape 1 roll Holding layouts together
Elastic band, hair tie or Velcro strap 2 Attaching the panel to the forearm
Printed text-size test strip (on the VLE) 1 Checking label legibility at wrist distance
Phone camera 1 Photographing each iteration for your journal
TipSizes to start from
  • Buttons: at least 2 cm × 2 cm (the lecture’s minimum), with gaps of at least 0.5 cm between them.
  • Item count: no more than 6 to 8 items.
  • Text: the lecture’s minimum of 24 pt at 1 m scales with distance. A wrist menu sits about 40 cm from the eyes, so its labels need to be at least 10 pt, and larger is safer.
  1. Sketch at Scale
    • Strap a piece of card to the inner forearm with an elastic band, about where a watch face would sit. Raise the wrist, palm up, into the position from the lecture’s wrist-menu pattern.
    • Measure the distance from the card to the eyes with the metre rule and write it down.
    • Cut a card panel to the size your wrist menu would occupy, and lay out your buttons on it with sticky notes. Most first designs are far too large for a forearm. Measure, do not estimate.
  2. Test the Reach
    • With the panel on one arm, have your partner press each button with the index finger of the other hand. Check that every control is reachable without shoulder strain and without twisting the wrist past comfortable.
    • Check legibility at the measured distance using the text-size test strip. Every label must be readable without leaning in.
    • Hold the pose for 30 seconds and note when the raised arm starts to tire. That is the menu’s time budget.
  3. Finalise
    • Ink the final layout on fresh card with dimensions (panel size, button sizes, gaps, text size) and photograph it before you build anything in Unity.
NoteTaking it into Unity later

When you build the menu in the lab, Unity Learn’s Create with VR 2.4 User Interface shows how to set up world-space UI that works with the XR Interaction Toolkit. Your dimensioned paper prototype gives you the canvas size in metres.

Expected Outcome

Two working locomotion schemes with exposed comfort settings, a record of peer comfort ratings before and after tuning, and a dimensioned paper prototype of a wrist menu validated for reach and legibility.

TipHint

Your own comfort is not evidence. Developers build tolerance to locomotion discomfort within days, which is exactly why the ratings that matter here are somebody else’s.
